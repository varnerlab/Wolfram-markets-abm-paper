\begin{abstract}
Financial markets crash without proportionate external shocks, yet most agent-based models
either assume global (mean-field) coupling that erases the spatial structure of contagion,
or employ ad hoc behavioral rules that resist systematic analysis. We introduce a minimal
model in which traders on a two-dimensional grid follow deterministic Wolfram totalistic
cellular automaton (CA) rules with three states---Buy, Hold, Sell---and update from their
four cardinal neighbors. Boundary agents inject real market sentiment derived from QQQ ETF
minute-bar data via a Bollinger Band filter; interior agents are governed by one of two
rules, a majority/herding rule and a contrarian/destabilizer rule, whose mixture fraction~$f$
is the single control parameter. The model exhibits a sharp first-order phase transition at
a critical minority fraction $f_c \approx 0.145$: below this threshold the market is stable,
while above it the system abruptly locks into a sell-dominated regime. At the transition,
sell cascades nucleate from localized seeds and propagate as spatially coherent wavefronts,
a contagion mechanism that is invisible to mean-field treatments and that connects naturally
to the tipping-point phenomenology observed in real markets. Because the full rule space
($3^9 = 19{,}683$ rules) is finite and enumerable, the framework enables systematic
screening of agent behaviors---a property unavailable in models with continuous parameters.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================================

Financial markets crash. They crash without proportionate external shocks, without advance
warning from traditional risk metrics, and with a statistical regularity---heavy-tailed
return distributions, volatility clustering, and transient negative
autocorrelation---that is remarkably consistent across instruments, geographic markets,
and sampling frequencies~\cite{cont2001empirical,mandelbrot1963variation,sornette2017why}.
A natural question arises: \emph{what is the minimal microscopic mechanism that is sufficient
to produce these phenomena?} If the answer involves the collective dynamics of interacting
agents rather than exogenous shocks, then the spatial structure of those
interactions---who talks to whom, and how far signals travel---matters at least as much as
the decision rules themselves~\cite{battiston2016complexity}.

The ingredients for an answer already exist, though they have not been assembled. On one
side, a generation of agent-based models (ABMs) has demonstrated that heterogeneous
populations of chartists and fundamentalists can reproduce fat tails and clustered
volatility~\cite{lux1999scaling,bak1997price}, that herding on random graphs is sufficient
to generate power-law-tailed returns~\cite{cont2000herd}, and that minority-game
dynamics~\cite{challet1997minority} and adaptive strategy
switching~\cite{farmer2002price,hommes2006heterogeneous} produce non-Gaussian fluctuations
without any exogenous noise assumption. On the other side, a mature literature on lattice
and spin models---Ising models with market
coupling~\cite{bornholdt2001ising,krawiecki2002volatility}, opinion dynamics on
chains~\cite{sznajd2002simple}, and the three-state speculative model of
Kaizoji~\cite{kaizoji2002speculative}---has shown that nearest-neighbor interactions on a
regular grid can produce phase transitions between ordered (crash) and disordered (efficient)
regimes. Meanwhile, cellular automata (CA) have been applied to financial
markets~\cite{wei2003cellular,qiu2007cellular,yang2019financial}, but only with ad hoc rule
designs that resist systematic comparison. What is missing is a framework that combines
\emph{local} spatial dynamics on a regular lattice with a \emph{complete, enumerable} space
of behavioral rules and a \emph{direct} connection to real market data.

In this paper, we close that gap. We model agent interactions on a two-dimensional grid
governed by Wolfram totalistic CA rules~\cite{wolfram2002new}, in which each agent occupies
one cell, maintains one of three states---Buy, Hold, or Sell---and updates deterministically
from the states of its four cardinal neighbors. The totalistic rule framework provides two
properties that are unavailable in prior financial ABMs: strictly local information
propagation, so that sell cascades must spread from neighbor to neighbor as spatially resolved
wavefronts; and a finite, enumerable rule space ($3^9 = 19{,}683$ rules), enabling systematic
screening of agent behaviors rather than ad hoc rule design. We study two complementary
configurations (Figure~\ref{fig:schematic}). In \textbf{Model~A} (open), boundary agents
observe real QQQ ETF minute-bar data via a Bollinger Band filter, injecting exogenous market
sentiment that validates the CA against empirical time series. In \textbf{Model~B} (closed),
the boundary agents respond to an endogenous price formed from the grid's own net order
imbalance, creating a reflexive feedback loop that produces crashes without any external input.
The single control parameter is the minority (contrarian) agent fraction~$f$, and the central
result is a sharp first-order phase transition at a critical threshold $f_c \approx 0.145$:
below $f_c$ the market is stable; above $f_c$ the system abruptly locks into a sell-dominated
regime. The transition is discontinuous, occurring over $\Delta f < 0.01$, consistent with a
tipping-point mechanism rather than a gradual crossover~\cite{scheffer2009early}. At the
transition, sell cascades nucleate from localized seeds and propagate as coherent spatial
wavefronts---a contagion mechanism that is invisible to mean-field treatments and that
connects naturally to the growing body of work on early-warning signals for critical
transitions in complex systems~\cite{scheffer2009early,battiston2016complexity}.

Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work on ABMs, lattice models, and CA in finance.
Section~\ref{sec:background} introduces the CA rule framework and edge-agent decision model.
Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the simulation procedure for Models~A and~B.
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the phase diagram and grid dynamics, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ============================================================================

Our work lies at the intersection of three research traditions: agent-based models of
financial markets, lattice and spin models from statistical mechanics, and cellular
automata theory. We review each strand below and identify where existing approaches
stop short of the connection we propose.

\subsection{Agent-Based Models and Stylized Facts}
The quantitative study of financial market dynamics has been shaped by a tension between
tractable theoretical frameworks and the empirical complexity of observed data.
Mandelbrot~\cite{mandelbrot1963variation} demonstrated that speculative price changes
exhibit heavy tails far beyond Gaussian predictions; Fama~\cite{fama1970efficient}
showed that raw return series display negligible linear autocorrelation, consistent with
the efficient markets hypothesis; yet Schwert~\cite{schwert1989volatility} documented
that the \emph{magnitude} of returns exhibits substantial positive autocorrelation
decaying slowly over time---large price changes tend to be followed by large changes
regardless of sign, a pattern known as \emph{volatility clustering}. Bouchaud and
Potters~\cite{bouchaud2002apparent} further documented the asymmetric response of
volatility to positive versus negative returns (the leverage effect). Cont~\cite{cont2001empirical}
provides a systematic review confirming these regularities across asset classes, geographic
markets, and sampling frequencies. Collectively, leptokurtosis, the absence of return
predictability, and volatility clustering define the benchmark against which any model of
market dynamics must be evaluated.

Agent-based models offer a bottom-up route to these stylized facts: rather than assuming
the statistical structure of returns, they derive it from the simulated interaction of
heterogeneous agents. The Santa Fe Artificial Stock Market~\cite{lebaron2000agent} established
the template of adaptive traders interacting through a centralized order book. Lux and
Marchesi~\cite{lux1999scaling} showed that a two-type fundamentalist-chartist population
reproduces heavy tails and volatility clustering as emergent properties, a result that
established ABMs as a credible framework for studying market dynamics. Cont and
Bouchaud~\cite{cont2000herd} demonstrated that a simple random-graph herding structure
alone is sufficient to generate fat-tailed return distributions, highlighting the role of
coordination---rather than agent sophistication---in generating extreme events. The minority
game of Challet and Zhang~\cite{challet1997minority} introduced the paradigm of
bounded-rational agents competing via strategic coordination, a framework subsequently shown
to produce non-Gaussian fluctuations; Farmer and Joshi~\cite{farmer2002price} demonstrated
that standard trading strategies simultaneously deployed by many agents endogenously generate
volatility clustering and excess kurtosis without any exogenous noise assumption; and Chen,
Tan, and Zheng~\cite{chen2015herding} showed that multi-level herding simultaneously
reproduces sector structure and volatility clustering. Harras and
Sornette~\cite{harras2012noise} modeled myopic adapting agents whose local herding behavior
inflates bubbles that eventually collapse, connecting the ABM literature to crash dynamics.

On the theoretical side, Hommes~\cite{hommes2006heterogeneous} surveys heterogeneous agent
models incorporating adaptive expectations and evolutionary strategy selection;
LeBaron~\cite{lebaron2006agent} provides a systematic treatment of agent-based computational
finance; Arthur~\cite{arthur2021complexity} argues that heterogeneous agent interactions give
rise to phenomena---persistent disequilibrium, emergent structure, tipping
points---inaccessible to equilibrium theory; and Farmer~\cite{farmer2025quantitative}
demonstrates that quantitative ABMs can produce time-series predictions competitive with
mainstream macroeconomic models. Battiston et al.~\cite{battiston2016complexity} make the
case that complexity science and ABMs should inform financial regulation. While mechanistically
compelling, these models share a persistent limitation: agents interact either globally
(mean-field coupling) or on ad hoc network topologies, making it difficult to disentangle the
contributions of local spatial contagion from global coordination. The present work addresses
this gap by placing agents on a regular lattice with strictly local CA rules, so that every
cascade must propagate neighbor-by-neighbor through physical space.

\subsection{Lattice Models, Spin Models, and Phase Transitions}
The mapping of financial agent interactions onto lattice models draws on the statistical
mechanics tradition, where the mathematical machinery for analyzing cooperative phenomena on
regular grids---phase transitions, critical exponents, universality classes---is well
developed. Ising-type models map agent states (buy/sell) onto spins and impose
nearest-neighbor coupling on a
lattice~\cite{stauffer2000generalization,chowdhury2000statistical}; these models naturally
capture local herding and can exhibit phase transitions between ordered (bubble/crash) and
disordered (efficient) regimes. Bornholdt~\cite{bornholdt2001ising} introduced a
two-dimensional Ising model with a global coupling term representing market impact, producing
intermittent bubbles and crashes from the interplay of local herding and global frustration
across scales. Sznajd-Weron and Weron~\cite{sznajd2002simple} proposed a one-dimensional
opinion model for price formation in which local consensus propagates outward, demonstrating
that even the simplest spatial rule can generate non-trivial collective dynamics. Krawiecki
et al.~\cite{krawiecki2002volatility} showed that attractor-bubbling dynamics on a lattice
generate volatility clustering with realistic scaling exponents. Kaizoji~\cite{kaizoji2002speculative}
extended the Ising framework to three states (buy/hold/sell) to model speculative activity,
which is the closest antecedent to our three-state CA formulation.

The crash literature provides the other essential ingredient. Sornette~\cite{sornette2017why}
argues that large crashes are the culmination of cooperative herding behavior and can be
modeled as critical points analogous to phase transitions in physical systems. The
self-organized criticality (SOC) framework of Bak, Tang, and
Wiesenfeld~\cite{bak1987selforganized} suggests that complex systems may naturally evolve
toward a critical state where scale-free avalanches occur without parameter tuning; Bak and
Paczuski~\cite{bak1996complexity} applied this idea to economic systems. Gabaix et
al.~\cite{gabaix2003theory} derive power-law tails from the size distribution of large
institutional trades, providing an alternative mechanism for heavy tails that does not require
agent heterogeneity. Scheffer et al.~\cite{scheffer2009early} propose generic early-warning
signals---rising autocorrelation and variance---for systems approaching a tipping point, a
framework that has been applied to ecosystems, climate, and financial
markets~\cite{battiston2016complexity}. Farmer et al.~\cite{farmer2005predictive} demonstrate
that even zero-intelligence agents can reproduce aspects of market microstructure when placed
in a realistic order-book mechanism, suggesting that the structure of interaction may matter
more than the sophistication of individual decision rules.

Metastability is not a metaphor we import into economics for the first time. Bouchaud's
broader program on crises and collective socio-economic phenomena already casts market
crashes in the language of statistical mechanics, treating them as barrier-crossing
transitions between metastable equilibria governed by the same slow-relaxation machinery
that organizes glasses and other disordered media~\cite{bouchaud2013crises}; and Sornette et
al.~\cite{sornette2003endogenous}, decomposing shocks in complex networks into endogenous and
exogenous components---validated empirically not on markets directly but on the cleaner
testbed of book-sale-rank cascades---show that self-generated shocks carry identifiable
precursory and relaxation fingerprints, separable from externally triggered ones---a
decomposition that presumes exactly the internal-dynamics picture we make explicit at the
agent level. Neither account, however,
supplies a setting in which the barrier, the waiting time, and the escape velocity can be
measured directly rather than merely invoked. A second, empirical literature studies the
interventions meant to arrest such escapes on real markets: Brunnermeier and
Pedersen~\cite{brunnermeier2009market} formalize the liquidity spiral in which funding shocks
and market illiquidity reinforce one another; Kirilenko et al.~\cite{kirilenko2017flash}
reconstruct the 2010 Flash Crash trade by trade to show how high-frequency intermediation
amplified, rather than absorbed, a single large sell order; Subrahmanyam~\cite{subrahmanyam1994circuit}
shows theoretically that circuit breakers can exacerbate the very volatility they are meant to
curb, as traders accelerate their trades to beat the anticipated halt; and Farhi and
Tirole~\cite{farhi2012collective} demonstrate that the anticipation of systemic bailouts
itself induces the correlated risk-taking---collective moral hazard---that later makes the
bailout necessary. Each of these studies takes its crisis and its intervention as
historically given, so the counterfactual---what the same market would have done absent the
circuit breaker, the injection, the bailout---can only be inferred, never rerun. The
contribution of this paper sits at the join of these two literatures: a model minimal enough
for us to measure the barrier and run the counterfactual intervention exactly.

Our approach generalizes the Ising lattice in two ways: (i)~we replace the single coupling
constant with Wolfram totalistic CA rules, which provide a richer and fully enumerable space
of $3^9 = 19{,}683$ local update functions; and (ii)~we couple the lattice to an external
or endogenous price signal via boundary agents, enabling direct validation against real market
data. The phase transition we observe is discontinuous (first-order) rather than continuous
(second-order), and occurs at a sharp critical minority fraction~$f_c$ rather than emerging
parameter-free as in SOC models.

\subsection{Cellular Automata in Complex Systems and Finance}
Wolfram~\cite{wolfram1984universality} classified one-dimensional CA into four behavioral
classes---fixed points, periodic orbits, spatial chaos, and complex/undecidable
dynamics---and later provided an exhaustive treatment of the
subject~\cite{wolfram2002new}. Packard and Wolfram~\cite{packard1985two} extended this
classification to two-dimensional totalistic rules on Von~Neumann neighborhoods,
demonstrating rich pattern-formation dynamics including self-replication, spiral waves,
and spatial chaos. The classification program reveals that even deterministic,
strictly local rules can produce dynamics ranging from trivially ordered to computationally
universal, a property that makes CA attractive as a modeling framework wherever the goal
is to understand how microscopic rules generate macroscopic complexity.

The application of CA to financial markets, however, remains sparse relative to the broader
ABM literature. Wei et al.~\cite{wei2003cellular} proposed a CA model for securities markets
in which agent states evolve based on local majority rules and demonstrated qualitative
agreement with empirical return distributions. Qiu et al.~\cite{qiu2007cellular} developed a
CA model specifically targeting abnormal fluctuations, showing that local interaction rules
can produce heavy-tailed returns. Yang et al.~\cite{yang2019financial} simulated financial
markets using CA with heterogeneous update rules and showed that the ratio of contrarian to
trend-following agents controls the transition between calm and volatile regimes---a result
qualitatively consistent with our phase diagram, though their model uses a different rule
framework and does not characterize the transition as a sharp phase boundary. None of these
studies uses the full Wolfram totalistic rule framework, and none couples the CA to real
market data or resolves the spatial structure of crash cascades.

The present work differs from prior CA-based financial models in three respects. First, we
embed agent dynamics in the complete Wolfram totalistic rule space ($3^9$ rules), enabling
systematic enumeration and comparison rather than ad hoc rule design. Second, we couple the
CA to real market data (Model~A) or an endogenous reflexive price (Model~B) via boundary
agents, bridging the gap between abstract CA dynamics and empirical financial time series.
Third, we identify a sharp first-order phase transition at a critical minority fraction and
resolve its spatial structure---nucleation of sell cascades from localized seeds followed by
wavefront propagation---connecting the CA dynamics to the tipping-point framework of
Scheffer et al.~\cite{scheffer2009early}.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}
% ============================================================================

This section establishes the formal ingredients of the model: the CA rule framework that
governs interior agent dynamics (Section~\ref{sec:ca-rules}), the Bollinger Band filter
that translates price signals into edge-agent states (Section~\ref{sec:edge-agents}), and
the specific rules used in this study (Section~\ref{sec:rule-taxonomy}).

\subsection{Three-State Totalistic Cellular Automata}
\label{sec:ca-rules}
We consider a two-dimensional grid of $N = N_r \times N_c$ agents, each in one of three
states: Buy~($s=0$), Hold~($s=1$), or Sell~($s=2$). At each discrete time step, every
interior agent updates its state deterministically based on the states of its four
cardinal (Von~Neumann) neighbors. For an agent at position $(r,c)$ with
neighbor states $\{s_N, s_S, s_E, s_W\}$, the neighborhood sum is:
\begin{equation}
  c_{r,c} = s_N + s_S + s_E + s_W \in \{0, 1, \ldots, 8\}.
  \label{eq:neighborhood-sum}
\end{equation}
A \emph{totalistic rule} is a mapping $\mathcal{R}: \{0,1,\ldots,8\} \to \{0,1,2\}$ that
assigns a next state to each of the nine possible neighborhood sums. Since each of the
nine outputs can take three values, there are $3^9 = 19{,}683$ distinct rules---each
identified by its decimal index:
\begin{equation}
  \text{Rule index} = \sum_{j=0}^{8} a_j \cdot 3^j,
  \label{eq:rule-index}
\end{equation}
where $a_j = \mathcal{R}(j)$ is the next state when the neighborhood sum equals~$j$.
The key structural observation is that this rule space is finite, enumerable, and
complete: every possible deterministic, translation-invariant, totalistic update function
on the Von~Neumann neighborhood is represented exactly once.

\subsection{Edge Agent Decision Model}
\label{sec:edge-agents}
Boundary cells serve as \emph{edge agents} that inject external information into the
grid (Figure~\ref{fig:schematic}A, green cells). A fraction $f_e$ of boundary cells,
selected uniformly at random with a fixed seed, are designated as edge agents; the
remaining boundary cells follow the same CA rule as interior agents.

Each edge agent classifies the current price $P_\tau$ relative to an exponential moving
average (EMA) and its associated volatility band:
\begin{align}
  \hat{S}_\tau &= \hat{S}_{\tau-1} + \alpha\bigl(P_\tau - \hat{S}_{\tau-1}\bigr), \label{eq:ema} \\
  \hat{\sigma}_\tau &= \hat{\sigma}_{\tau-1} + \alpha\bigl(|P_\tau - \hat{S}_\tau| - \hat{\sigma}_{\tau-1}\bigr), \label{eq:ema-vol}
\end{align}
where $\alpha = 1/60$ corresponds to a 60-minute smoothing window. The decision rule
is a three-way classifier:
\begin{equation}
  e_\tau = \begin{cases}
    0 \;\text{(Buy)}  & \text{if } P_\tau < \hat{S}_\tau - k\,\hat{\sigma}_\tau, \\
    1 \;\text{(Hold)} & \text{if } \hat{S}_\tau - k\,\hat{\sigma}_\tau \le P_\tau \le \hat{S}_\tau + k\,\hat{\sigma}_\tau, \\
    2 \;\text{(Sell)} & \text{if } P_\tau > \hat{S}_\tau + k\,\hat{\sigma}_\tau,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:edge-state}
\end{equation}
where $k$ controls the width of the hold band. When the price is within $k$ standard
deviations of its moving average, the edge agent holds; when the price breaks out above
(below) the band, it sells (buys). This is a standard mean-reversion signal widely used
in technical analysis, and its simplicity is deliberate: the edge agents provide a minimal,
interpretable channel through which real market information enters the grid.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
% ============================================================================

The background section established the CA rule framework, the edge-agent decision model,
and the specific rules under study. We now describe the two simulation configurations
and the phase-sweep protocol used to map the crash transition.

\subsection{Model~A: Open (Boundary-Driven) Simulation}
\label{sec:model-a}
In Model~A (Figure~\ref{fig:schematic}C), edge agents observe real market
data---specifically, one-minute OHLC bars for the QQQ ETF (Invesco QQQ Trust, 2024
calendar year, $T = 23{,}790$ time steps). At each step~$\tau$ three operations occur in
sequence: (i)~the typical price $P_\tau = (H_\tau + L_\tau + C_\tau)/3$ is computed from
the OHLC bar; (ii)~each edge agent evaluates Eq.~\eqref{eq:edge-state} using the online
EMA and volatility from Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ema}--\eqref{eq:ema-vol}; and (iii)~all interior
agents update simultaneously via their assigned CA rule. The grid is initialized to
all-Hold ($s=1$), so that any Buy or Sell activity must originate from the boundary and
propagate inward.

For the heterogeneous (crash) model, a fraction~$f$ of interior agents are assigned
Rule~19286 (contrarian) uniformly at random, while the remaining $1-f$ agents use
Rule~19280 (majority). The minority agent positions are fixed for the duration of the
simulation (quenched disorder), so that the only control parameter is the mixture
fraction~$f$.

\paragraph{Simulation parameters.}
The default grid is $256 \times 256$ ($N = 65{,}536$ agents). Edge agents comprise
25\% of boundary cells ($f_e = 0.25$, yielding ${\sim}512$ active edge agents), selected
with a fixed random seed for reproducibility. The EMA smoothing parameter is
$\alpha = 1/60$ (a 60-minute window) and the band width is $k = 1.0$ standard deviations.

\subsection{Model~B: Closed (Endogenous Price) Simulation}
\label{sec:model-b}
In Model~B (Figure~\ref{fig:schematic}D), there is no external price feed; instead, the
price is generated endogenously from the grid's own collective state, and every trading
cell---each interior agent together with each active edge agent---now carries a \emph{book}
that gates whether its sentiment can be acted upon: a cash balance initialized to
$\texttt{initial\_cash}$ (the sweep variable, default~$1{,}000$), a share inventory
initialized to $10$ shares acquired at the reference cost basis $P_0 = 100$, and a running
average cost basis that tracks the price paid across subsequent fills. Sentiment still flows
by the same totalistic rule as in Model~A---this is what keeps the two models' interiors
directly comparable---but a cell now expresses that sentiment through orders: a cell in the
Buy state submits a one-share buy order every step it remains in that state, a cell in the
Sell state submits a one-share sell order, and a Hold cell submits nothing.

Orders do not clear against one another; they fill at the standing quote $P_{\tau-1}$
against an infinite-inventory market maker in the spirit of the price-impact accounting of
Kyle~\cite{kyle1985continuous} and the market-clearing conventions of Farmer and
collaborators~\cite{farmer2005predictive}. A buy fills if and only if the cell holds cash
$\ge P$; a sell fills if and only if the cell holds at least one share and, when the
disposition toggle is on, the standing quote exceeds the cell's average cost basis,
$P > \bar{c}_i$. This cost-basis sell rule is the \emph{disposition effect} of Shefrin and
Statman~\cite{shefrin1985disposition}, the reluctance to realize losses that Odean later
documented in the trading records of individual investors~\cite{odean1998investors}; we
treat it not as a modeling assumption but as a behavioral bias and hence a sweep factor,
toggled on and off across campaigns. Every order that the book cannot honor is tallied as a
\emph{suppressed} order rather than executed: budget constraints suppress order \emph{flow},
never the underlying CA states, so the interior's sentiment field evolves identically to
Model~A while its market footprint is throttled by whatever cash and inventory each cell
happens to hold.

Because fills, not states, now drive the price, the imbalance is measured on executed flow
rather than on the raw sentiment census:
\begin{equation}
  \varphi_\tau = \frac{(\text{executed buys})_\tau - (\text{executed sells})_\tau}{N_{\text{traders}}},
  \qquad N_{\text{traders}} = N_{\text{interior}} + N_{\text{active edge}},
  \label{eq:imbalance}
\end{equation}
and the price updates multiplicatively from this executed imbalance,
\begin{equation}
  P_\tau = P_{\tau-1} \cdot \exp\!\bigl(\lambda\,\varphi_\tau\bigr),
  \label{eq:price-update}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda > 0$ is the price-impact parameter controlling how strongly the aggregate
order flow moves the quote. Edge agents are full participants in $\varphi$---their fills
enter the numerator on the same footing as interior fills---and, to break the all-or-none
lockstep that a single shared band would impose, each edge cell draws its own band
half-width $k_i \sim U\!\bigl(k(1-\delta),\, k(1+\delta)\bigr)$, where $\delta$ is the
band-spread parameter; the edge cells then evaluate Eq.~\eqref{eq:edge-state} against the
newly generated price $P_\tau$, with the online EMA and volatility recursions of
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ema}--\eqref{eq:ema-vol} observing that same new price. The result is a fully
self-contained loop: the grid's executed orders generate the price, the price drives the
edge agents, and the edge agents inject signals back into the grid.

To probe that loop causally we expose a deterministic intervention hook---a function of the
current step and the full collection of books, $(t, \text{books})$, invoked at the top of
step~$t$ before any orders execute---through which we implement mid-run liquidity
injections and withdrawals by scaling every cell's cash by a common factor. The hook takes
no randomness of its own, so the same seed still yields an identical trajectory up to the
moment of intervention; this same-seed, identical-trajectory contract is precisely what the
paired counterfactuals of Results~II rely on to attribute any downstream divergence to the
liquidity shock alone.

\subsection{Phase Sweep Protocol}
\label{sec:phase-sweep}
To map the crash transition, we sweep the minority fraction $f$ from $0.0$ to $0.50$ in
two passes. A coarse sweep with $\Delta f = 0.05$ covers the full range; a fine-grained
sweep with $\Delta f = 0.01$ resolves the transition region $f \in [0.08, 0.22]$.
For each value of~$f$, the full $T$-step price series drives a $128 \times 128$ grid
(chosen for computational efficiency; the transition location is robust to grid size),
and we record the sell fraction at every time step. Three summary statistics characterize
each run: mean sell fraction, peak sell fraction, and crash frequency (the fraction of
time steps at which the sell fraction exceeds~$0.50$).

\subsection{Model~B Nucleation Campaigns}
\label{sec:mb-protocols}
The endogenous-price machinery of Model~B lets us treat a market crash as a nucleation
event and interrogate it with a family of purpose-built campaigns; unless a campaign states
otherwise, all of them share one configuration---a $64 \times 64$ grid, price impact
$\lambda = 0.02$, nominal band half-width $k = 1$, band spread $\delta = 0.5$, the
disposition rule switched off, an initial quote $P_0 = 100$, and deterministic seeds---and
they differ only in the minority fraction~$f$, the initial cash, and the horizon over which
we let the loop run, which is $1{,}000$ steps for the seed sweep, $1{,}500$ steps for the
budget-toggle and boundary-versus-bulk campaigns, and $2{,}000$ steps for the escape-time
campaign. Two definitions, fixed once here and reused throughout Results~II, make the
campaigns compose: we label sell clusters by breadth-first search over the $4$-connected
Sell cells of the grid, and we define the crash onset $t_{\mathrm{on}}$ as the first step at
which the largest sell cluster reaches $5\%$ of the grid. Cluster labeling runs on the
full grid in every campaign but one---the boundary-versus-bulk birth analysis instead labels
on the interior alone, so that a cluster attached to the active-edge ring can be told apart
from one born in the bulk---and because onset and cluster size are measured identically
everywhere else, front velocities and onset times reported by different analyses can be read
on a common footing.

The seed sweep establishes that the qualitative fate of a run is set by its budget regime
rather than its particular disorder realization: we run three configurations---a
\emph{stable} regime at $f = 0.10$ with cash $1{,}000$, a \emph{freeze} regime at $f = 0.20$
with cash $500$, and a \emph{collapse} regime at $f = 0.20$ with cash $5{,}000$---across $20$
seeds each. The budget toggle then turns the deterministic hook into a controlled experiment:
for each seed we run paired counterfactuals in which, exactly at $t_{\mathrm{on}}$, every
cell's cash is scaled by $\times 10$ (an injection) or by $\times 0.1$ or $\times 0.01$ (two
withdrawal doses), with the pre-toggle histories asserted bit-identical between the members
of a pair so that any divergence dates strictly from the shock; we then measure outcomes in an
onset-aligned window of $W = 400$ steps and take the front velocity to be the endpoint
difference quotient---the change in largest-cluster fraction between onset and
onset$+W$, divided by $W$---rather than a fitted slope; a pair is excluded from every
arm when the control run had no onset at all or when $t_{\mathrm{on}}$ fell too late to
leave a full window before the horizon ($t_{\mathrm{on}} >$ horizon $- W$). The
boundary-versus-bulk campaign
asks where the winning cluster is born: we track the founding cell of the onset cluster and
compare its location against the analytic null for spatially uniform births, under which the
fraction of births falling within distance~$2$ of the boundary is~$0.125$, and we complement
this observational arm with an interventional one that stamps a $3 \times 3$ sell patch onto
the grid at boundary-adjacent, mid, and center placements, each paired against an unstamped
control.

The escape-time campaign measures how the waiting time to nucleate scales with the distance
from criticality, and it does so at three cash levels, $\text{cash} \in \{500,\,1{,}000,\,
5{,}000\}$. A pilot pass sweeps $f \in \{0.12,\,0.15,\,0.18,\,0.20,\,0.25,\,0.30\}$ at $20$
seeds; having localized the onset cliff to the interval between $0.15$ and $0.18$ at all
three cash levels, a refinement pass adds $f \in \{0.156,\,0.162,\,0.168,\,0.174,\,0.177\}$
at $40$ seeds. We take the escape time to be $\tau = t_{\mathrm{on}}$, right-censored at the
$2{,}000$-step horizon and never discarded, so that runs which never nucleate still inform
the estimate; per-$f$ typical times $\tilde\tau$ are therefore Kaplan--Meier medians of the
censored data, and we fit the Kramers-like barrier law $\ln \tilde\tau = A + B/(f - f_0)$
separately at each cash level by nonlinear least squares on those censored medians with the
pseudo-critical point $f_0$ left free, obtaining $95\%$ confidence intervals on
$(A, B, f_0)$ from $2{,}000$ bootstrap resamples of the seeds within each~$f$.

A companion
velocity--suppression dose-response holds $f = 0.20$ and extends the cash ladder with the
levels $250$ and $2{,}000$ so that five doses are probed at $20$ seeds each; for every run we
form, over the window $[t_{\mathrm{on}} + 50,\, t_{\mathrm{on}} + 400]$, a front velocity as
the OLS slope of the front extent and a suppression load as the mean \emph{absolute} number
of suppressed buy orders per step, and we test their association by Spearman rank correlation
with a $10{,}000$-draw permutation $p$-value, excluding and separately counting any run that
is censored before onset or whose window endpoint $t_{\mathrm{on}} + 400$ would exceed the
horizon.

Finally, to place these targeted campaigns against the model's full parameter dependence, we
draw on a large factorial sweep whose crash statistics anchor the regime discussion of
Section~\ref{sec:results}: the full crossing of $f \in \{0,\,0.1,\,0.15,\,0.2,\,0.3\}$,
$\lambda \in \{0.005,\,0.01,\,0.02,\,0.05\}$, $\text{cash} \in \{500,\,1{,}000,\,5{,}000\}$,
disposition $\in \{\text{off},\,\text{on}\}$, band spread $\delta \in \{0,\,0.5,\,0.9\}$, and
band half-width $k \in \{0.5,\,1,\,2\}$ yields $1{,}080$ configurations which, replicated
across $10$ seeds of $2{,}000$ steps, comprise $10{,}800$ runs; the full design and its
variance decomposition are deferred to Appendix~\ref{app:stage2}.

\section{Results I: Model~A, the Clean Nucleation Baseline}
\label{sec:results}
% ============================================================================

Model~A is the unconstrained baseline of the nucleation argument: agents trade without
budgets, prices arrive from outside the market, and nothing in the dynamics can throttle
an order once a rule calls for it, so a sell nucleus that manages to form runs unopposed
to completion. We establish that clean phenomenon in two steps---the spatiotemporal
anatomy of a single crash, then the phase diagram that locates its threshold---before
Section~\ref{sec:theory} quantifies the barrier that guards it and Model~B
(Section~\ref{sec:results-b}) constrains the growth that follows it.

\subsection{Grid Dynamics: Stable vs.\ Crash Regimes}
We simulated the $256 \times 256$ agent grid under Model~A for three values of the
minority fraction---$f = 0$ (homogeneous), $f = 0.15$ (near-critical), and $f = 0.20$
(supercritical)---and recorded grid snapshots at four logarithmically spaced time points
to capture both the early transient and the late-time steady state
(Figure~\ref{fig:fig2}).

In the homogeneous model ($f = 0$, top row), the grid reached a dynamic steady state
dominated by Buy (blue) and Sell (orange) agents in a fine-grained spatial mixture; the
pattern fluctuated locally but never exhibited large-scale coordination, and the sell
fraction remained near its equilibrium value of ${\sim}0.26$ throughout the simulation.
At $f = 0.15$ (middle row)---just above the critical threshold---the system initially
resembled the homogeneous case, but at $t = 8{,}236$ we observed a sell cascade that
nucleated from a localized seed and propagated outward as a spatially coherent wavefront.
The orange region grew concentrically until it engulfed the grid by $t = 23{,}790$,
which suggests a contagion mechanism qualitatively different from the instantaneous
global crashes produced by mean-field models: the crash had a \emph{spatial history},
starting at one location, spreading through a physical medium, and arriving at different
parts of the grid at different times (Figure~\ref{fig:fig2}, middle row). This
sequence---a localized seed, a coherent front, a spatial history---is
nucleation-and-growth in the classical sense. At $f = 0.20$
(bottom row), the cascade occurred earlier and more rapidly; the grid was already
predominantly sell-dominated by $t = 2{,}851$, consistent with a system deep in the
supercritical regime.

\subsection{Phase Diagram}
We swept the minority fraction~$f$ from $0.0$ to $0.50$ and computed three summary
statistics---mean sell fraction, peak sell fraction, and crash frequency---for each run
(Figure~\ref{fig:fig3}). All three order parameters exhibited a sharp discontinuous
transition at a critical threshold $f_c \approx 0.145$ (dashed vertical line).

Below $f_c$, the system remained stable: the mean sell fraction hovered near $0.26$
(close to the $1/3$ expected for a uniform random mixture of three states), the peak sell
fraction never exceeded $0.30$, and no crashes occurred. Above $f_c$, the system abruptly
entered a sell-dominated regime: the mean sell fraction jumped to ${\sim}0.50$ at
$f = 0.15$ and saturated near $0.91$ by $f = 0.20$; crash frequency exceeded 95\%
(Figure~\ref{fig:fig3}C). The transition width was less than $\Delta f = 0.01$, and the
jump in the order parameter was discontinuous, both hallmarks of a first-order phase
transition rather than the smooth crossover that would characterize a second-order
(Ising-type) critical point.

These results suggest a clear economic interpretation: a small minority of contrarian
agents was harmless, because Rule~19280 (majority) agents damped local sell signals
through herding toward the dominant state. But once the contrarian fraction
exceeded~$f_c$, Rule~19286 agents were numerous enough to nucleate sell clusters that the
majority-rule agents could no longer absorb; instead, the herding mechanism
\emph{amplified} the cascade, because the local majority had tipped from buy to sell. The
result was a coordination failure---a flash crash triggered by the interaction between two
deterministic rules, without any stochastic component, learning, or adaptive strategy
switching. In the language that Section~\ref{sec:theory} makes precise, the stability
below $f_c$---at least above the threshold $f_0$ where the nucleation barrier becomes
infinite---was in fact \emph{metastability}: sell clusters still formed, but each
died before reaching the critical size at which the herding mechanism turns from damper
to amplifier. The crash was an emergent property of the rule mixture, not of any
individual rule.

\section{Theory: The Nucleation Barrier}
\label{sec:theory}
% ============================================================================

Results~I established \emph{what} happens: above a sharp threshold in the contrarian
fraction, the market abandons its mixed attractor through a discontinuous transition
that propagates as spatially coherent fronts rather than as an instantaneous global
failure. This section establishes \emph{why}. We proceed through a hierarchy of
approximations that progressively eliminates every non-spatial explanation---mean-field
theory fails, the pair approximation fails, and nucleation theory succeeds---and the
hierarchy terminates in a quantitative escape law,
$\ln\bar{\tau} = 2.78 + 0.389/(f - 0.101)$ ($R^2 = 0.991$), that assigns the
transition both a barrier and a divergence threshold. That law is the quantitative
claim Model~B must answer in Results~II: if budget constraints change the physics of
the transition, they must move this barrier.

\subsection{Potts Energy Mapping}
\label{sec:potts}

The majority/herding rule (19280) admits a natural interpretation as zero-temperature
dynamics of a three-state Potts model. We defined the Potts alignment energy per
nearest-neighbour bond as
\begin{equation}
  e \;=\; -\frac{1}{2N_{\mathrm{bonds}}}
       \sum_{\langle i,j\rangle} \delta(\sigma_i,\,\sigma_j),
  \label{eq:potts-energy}
\end{equation}
where the sum runs over all distinct nearest-neighbour pairs on the lattice and
$\delta$ is the Kronecker delta. In a perfectly aligned (single-state) configuration
$e = -1$; in a maximally disordered configuration $e \approx -1/3$.
For Rule~19280, the output at neighbourhood sum $c \in \{2,3\}$ is Buy, at
$c \in \{4,5\}$ is Hold, and at $c \in \{6,7,8\}$ is Sell---precisely the states that
maximise local alignment with the majority. The one exception is
$c = 0$ (all neighbours Buy), which outputs Sell rather than Buy. This anomaly means
that the all-Buy configuration is \emph{unstable}: every interior cell flips to Sell
in a single time step, and the all-Sell absorbing state ($c = 8 \to \text{Sell}$)
is the unique ground state at $e = -1$. The ``buy-dominated'' phase observed in
simulations is therefore not a static equilibrium but a \emph{dynamic mixed-state
attractor} with fractions $p_0 \approx 0.49$, $p_1 \approx 0.24$,
$p_2 \approx 0.26$ and $e \approx -0.37$---a metastable state separated from the
all-Sell ground state by a barrier that the deterministic majority dynamics alone
cannot cross.

The contrarian rule (19286), which differs only at $c = 1$ by outputting Sell instead
of Buy, acts as quenched disorder in this Potts framework: a fraction~$f$ of sites
follow a rule that locally \emph{opposes} alignment, creating sell-biased defects in
the majority-rule background.

We measured the Potts energy and the single-site Shannon entropy
$S_1 = -\sum_s \rho_s \ln \rho_s$ across the phase sweep on a $128 \times 128$
lattice with periodic boundaries. Below $f_c$ the mixed attractor persisted at
$e \approx -0.37$ and $S_1 \approx 1.06$ (near the maximum $\ln 3 \approx 1.10$);
above $f_c$ the system collapsed to the all-Sell absorbing state at
$e \approx -0.93$ and $S_1 \approx 0.18$---a discontinuous drop characteristic of a
first-order transition.

\subsection{Mean-Field Analysis}
\label{sec:mean-field}

To isolate the role of spatial correlations, we derived a mean-field self-consistency
map for the mixed 19280/19286 population. The mean-field approximation replaces the
lattice with a well-mixed population in which each agent's four neighbours are drawn
independently from the global state distribution $(p_0, p_1, p_2)$, where $p_s$
denotes the fraction of agents in state~$s$ ($p_0 + p_1 + p_2 = 1$). Because each
neighbour contributes independently to the neighbourhood sum
$c = s_N + s_S + s_E + s_W$, the probability $\pi_k = \Pr(c = k)$ follows from
the multinomial convolution of four i.i.d.\ draws from $\{0,1,2\}$ with weights
$(p_0, p_1, p_2)$:
\begin{equation}
  \pi_k = \!\!\sum_{\substack{n_0+n_1+n_2=4 \\ n_1+2n_2=k}}\!\!
  \binom{4}{n_0,\,n_1,\,n_2}\,p_0^{n_0}\,p_1^{n_1}\,p_2^{n_2},
  \qquad k = 0,1,\ldots,8.
  \label{eq:pi-dist}
\end{equation}
The verified rule tables (Section~\ref{sec:rule-taxonomy}) assign next states as
follows: $c \in \{2,3\} \to \text{Buy}$; $c \in \{4,5\} \to \text{Hold}$;
$c \in \{0,6,7,8\} \to \text{Sell}$ (both rules agree at these values); and $c = 1$,
where the two rules diverge---Rule~19280 outputs Buy while Rule~19286 outputs Sell.
In a population with minority fraction~$f$, the mean-field map is therefore:
\begin{align}
  p_0' &= (1-f)\,\pi_1 + \pi_2 + \pi_3, \label{eq:mf-p0} \\
  p_1' &= \pi_4 + \pi_5, \label{eq:mf-p1} \\
  p_2' &= \pi_0 + f\,\pi_1 + \pi_6 + \pi_7 + \pi_8, \label{eq:mf-p2}
\end{align}
where primes denote the next-step fractions and $\pi_k$ depends on the current
$(p_0, p_2)$ through Eq.~\eqref{eq:pi-dist}.

We iterated Eqs.~\eqref{eq:mf-p0}--\eqref{eq:mf-p2} to convergence
($\lVert\mathbf{p}' - \mathbf{p}\rVert_1 < 10^{-14}$) from multiple initial
conditions and swept $f$ from $0$ to $1$. For every value of~$f$ the map admitted
two stable fixed points: (i)~a \emph{buy-dominated} equilibrium with
$p_0^* \approx 0.63$, $p_1^* \approx 0.18$, $p_2^* \approx 0.19$ at $f = 0$,
shifting smoothly to $p_0^* \approx 0.45$, $p_2^* \approx 0.23$ at $f = 1$; and
(ii)~the all-Sell absorbing state $p_2^* = 1$. The Jacobian at the buy-dominated
fixed point had complex eigenvalues with modulus $|\lambda| \approx 0.95$ (a stable
spiral), confirming local stability. At no value of~$f$ did the buy-dominated branch
disappear or cross the sell-majority threshold $p_2 > 0.50$. The mean-field map
predicted no phase transition whatsoever: the sell fraction increased by only
${\sim}0.04$ across the entire range of~$f$, a smooth monotone drift with no
discontinuity.

\subsection{Pair Approximation}
\label{sec:pair-approx}

We extended the mean-field analysis to the pair approximation, which tracks
nearest-neighbour pair densities $\rho_{ab} = \Pr(\sigma_i = a,\, \sigma_j = b)$
for adjacent sites $i, j$ rather than single-site marginals alone. In the pair
approximation closure~\cite{gutowitz1987local}, the three neighbours of a focal site
\emph{other} than its paired partner are drawn independently from the conditional
distribution $q(s \mid a) = \rho_{as} / \rho_a$, where
$\rho_a = \sum_b \rho_{ab}$ is the marginal density of state~$a$. For a synchronous
update, the new pair density is
\begin{equation}
  \rho'_{bb'} = \sum_{a,a'} \rho_{aa'}\;
    T_b(a, a')\; T_{b'}(a', a),
  \label{eq:pair-update}
\end{equation}
where $T_b(a, a')$ is the probability that a site in state~$a$ with paired neighbour
in state~$a'$ transitions to state~$b$; it is computed from the effective rule table
and the trinomial distribution of the remaining three neighbours' sum
(see Supplementary Material for the full derivation).

The pair approximation also predicted no phase transition. We computed the $6 \times 6$
Jacobian of the pair map~\eqref{eq:pair-update} evaluated at the uncorrelated
(mean-field) fixed point $\rho_{ab}^* = \rho_a^* \rho_b^*$ and found that its spectral
radius was strictly less than unity for all $f \in [0, 1]$---ranging from
$|\lambda_{\max}| \approx 0.95$ at $f = 0$ to $|\lambda_{\max}| \approx 0.71$ at
$f = 0.5$. A systematic search over correlated initial conditions (same-state
correlation $\varepsilon$ ranging from $0.1$ to $0.5$, both buy- and sell-biased)
confirmed that every trajectory converged to one of only two fixed points: the
uncorrelated buy-dominated state (identical to mean-field) or the all-Sell absorbing
state. Spatial mutual information $I = 2S_1 - S_2$ decayed to zero in all cases.

The failure of the pair approximation is significant: it establishes that the phase
transition cannot be understood as a growing instability at \emph{any} finite
correlation order. Both mean-field (zero-range correlations) and pair approximation
(nearest-neighbour correlations) see the same picture---two stable fixed points
separated by a barrier that never vanishes. The lattice transition must therefore
proceed by a qualitatively different mechanism: barrier crossing via a rare,
large-scale fluctuation.

\subsection{Nucleation Theory}
\label{sec:nucleation}

The successive failure of mean-field and pair approximation identifies the lattice
transition as a \emph{nucleation} phenomenon---the stochastic escape from a metastable
dynamic attractor over a free-energy barrier. We characterised this mechanism through
three measurements.

\paragraph{Metastable lifetime.}
We initialised $64 \times 64$ lattices from random states, equilibrated for 200~steps
to the mixed attractor, then recorded the time $\tau$ at which the sell fraction first
exceeded~$0.90$ (collapse to the absorbing state). For each value of~$f$ we ran 20
independent realisations with distinct random seeds for both initial conditions and
minority-agent placement. Below $f \approx 0.14$ the mixed state survived in all
realisations for $10^5$~steps; above $f \approx 0.155$ all realisations collapsed,
with the mean lifetime decreasing from $\bar{\tau} \approx 24{,}000$ at $f = 0.155$
to $\bar{\tau} \approx 86$ at $f = 0.30$.

\paragraph{Kramers escape scaling.}
The lifetime data were well described by the escape form of
Kramers~\cite{kramers1940brownian}, the classical framework for thermally activated
barrier crossing reviewed by H\"{a}nggi, Talkner, and
Borkovec~\cite{hanggi1990reaction}:
\begin{equation}
  \ln \bar{\tau} \;=\; a + \frac{B}{f - f_0},
  \label{eq:kramers}
\end{equation}
with best-fit parameters $a = 2.78$, $B = 0.389$, and $f_0 = 0.101$
($R^2 = 0.991$; $n = 12$ data points with all seeds collapsed). The divergence at
$f_0 \approx 0.10$ implies that the nucleation barrier becomes infinite below this
threshold: the mixed attractor is \emph{absolutely stable} for $f < f_0$, and no
amount of waiting will produce a collapse. The observed transition
$f_c \approx 0.15$--$0.16$ is not a sharp thermodynamic threshold but an
observation-time-dependent crossover---the value of~$f$ at which the nucleation time
first falls below the simulation horizon $T$.

Equation~\eqref{eq:kramers} predicts the effective $f_c$ at any desired time scale:
for example, $f_c(T = 10{,}000) \approx 0.162$ and $f_c(T = 1{,}000) \approx 0.195$,
consistent with the steepening of the phase boundary observed in the
simulations.

\paragraph{Mechanism: disorder-driven nucleation.}
We measured the spatial influence of an isolated contrarian agent embedded in an
otherwise all-majority lattice. The sell density at the contrarian's own site was
elevated ($\rho_2 \approx 0.42$ vs.\ the background $\rho_2 \approx 0.26$), but this
excess decayed to zero within one to two lattice spacings. Individual contrarians
therefore act as point-like sell seeds with negligible spatial range; the transition
requires the \emph{collective} effect of many such seeds. Sell cluster analysis
confirmed this: below $f_c$ the largest sell cluster in the mixed attractor contained
${\sim}30$--$60$~cells; at $f = 0.145$ transient clusters of ${\sim}400$~cells
appeared; and at $f = 0.16$ macroscopic clusters exceeding $1{,}800$~cells formed and
triggered system-wide collapse.

The nucleation mechanism is thus disorder-driven: the quenched random placement of
contrarian agents at density~$f$ creates local density fluctuations; at $f > f_0$,
rare regions with an anomalously high local contrarian density seed sell clusters that
exceed the critical size; the majority/herding rule then amplifies these seeds into
coherent wavefronts that engulf the lattice. The system-size dependence of $f_c$ was
weak ($f_c \approx 0.17$ at $L = 32$, $f_c \approx 0.16$ at $L = 128$), consistent
with a critical nucleus that is small relative to the lattice dimensions at all tested
sizes.

\paragraph{Summary.}
The theoretical hierarchy---mean-field $\to$ pair approximation $\to$ nucleation
theory---progressively narrows the mechanism responsible for the phase transition.
Mean-field theory, by erasing all spatial structure, misses the transition entirely.
The pair approximation, which restores nearest-neighbour correlations, fares no
better: the uncorrelated fixed point remains linearly stable at all~$f$, and the
spatial mutual information vanishes. Only the nucleation framework, which accounts for
rare large-scale fluctuations seeded by quenched contrarian disorder, correctly
predicts both the existence of the transition and the Kramers-like divergence of the
metastable lifetime. The Potts energy mapping provides the natural order parameter: the
energy discontinuity $\Delta e \approx 0.56$ at $f_c$, from the mixed attractor
($e \approx -0.37$) to the all-Sell ground state ($e \approx -0.93$), confirms the
first-order character of the transition and quantifies the barrier that the system must
overcome.

This barrier picture does more than explain Model~A; it makes three falsifiable
predictions for the budget-constrained model that follows. If nucleation survives the
introduction of finite budgets, escape times should still diverge as
$A + B/(f - f_0)$, the functional signature of barrier crossing; if budgets act on
nucleation itself, the effect must appear as a shift in the fitted pair
$(B, f_0)$---a taller barrier or a displaced divergence threshold; and if budgets act
elsewhere in the dynamics, the law should hold with the \emph{same} barrier while
growth-sensitive observables move instead. Results~II
(Section~\ref{sec:results-b}) tests exactly these predictions.

\section{Results II: Model~B, the Barrier under Budget Constraints}
\label{sec:results-b}
% ============================================================================

Results~I established Model~A as a clean nucleation baseline---a deterministic market
that lingers in a mixed attractor until quenched contrarian disorder seeds a sell cluster
large enough to grow into a lattice-spanning crash---and the theory developed alongside it
quantified the barrier that guards that transition; Results~II asks what finite budgets do
to this picture. Model~B retains the same two rules and the same disorder but couples them
to an endogenous price and a cash constraint that can throttle the very orders the rules
call for, and we interrogate the resulting \emph{arrested} nucleus in five steps: whether
the crash transition survives budgets at all (Section~\ref{sec:mb-transition}), whether the
nucleus that drives it remains a spatial object rather than a mean-field instability,
whether budgets move the nucleation barrier or merely gate the growth that follows it, where
in the market the budget constraint actually bites, and what the asymmetry we uncover there
implies for liquidity intervention.

\subsection{The Transition Survives Budgets}
\label{sec:mb-transition}
The first question---whether budgets destroy the transition or merely dress it---we settled
with the large Model~B factorial sweep described in Section~\ref{sec:mb-protocols}, a
$10{,}800$-run crossing of the minority fraction, the price impact, the cash endowment, the
disposition switch, and the two order-band parameters. The crash transition survived the
imposition of budgets intact: the sell-dominated regime appeared abruptly between
$f = 0.15$ and $f = 0.20$ at every price impact~$\lambda$ we tested, reproducing the
threshold that Model~A had located without any price feedback; and a variance decomposition
attributed $94$--$98\%$ of the outcome variance to the swept knobs and at most $5.4\%$ to the
disorder seed---so the fate of a Model~B run was fixed overwhelmingly by its budget regime,
not by the particular random placement of its contrarians.

What budgets changed was not whether the market crashed but how it crashed, and to see this
we classified every run by its late-time behavior into one of five candidate
regimes---inert, crash-prone, oscillatory, plateau, and drift, defined in
Appendix~\ref{app:stage2}---of which only two were ever realized: an oscillatory regime in
$6{,}589$ runs ($61\%$) and a crash-prone regime in the remaining $4{,}211$ ($39\%$). We
stress that ``crash-prone'' is a behavioral label rather than a statement about prices: a run
earned it whenever the Sell fraction stood at or above $0.5$ on at least $1\%$ of its
steps---a sustained majority-sell episode on the grid---and such an episode need not, and
usually did not, coincide with a large price drawdown.

That decoupling of grid state from price surfaced the first genuinely new phenomenon of
Model~B, the liquidity \emph{freeze}. The typical crash-prone run was not a collapse but a
sell-saturated stasis: its interior locked near a Sell fraction of $0.88$ while budget
exhaustion starved the order flow that would have moved the quote, so that the median maximum
drawdown across crash-prone runs was only about $5\%$ and fully half of them never traded
below the initial price at all (median price minimum $100.00$). True price collapses lived in
the tail of this distribution---$897$ of the $4{,}211$ crash-prone runs drove the price below
$90$, and the most violent of them, concentrated at $f = 0.2$, $\lambda = 0.05$, cash
$5{,}000$, and disposition off, first inflated $2.5$--$2.7\times$ bubbles and then crashed
them by $40$--$69\%$, with the disposition effect roughly doubling the return volatility
wherever it was switched on (a ratio of $2.40\times$). Figure~\ref{fig:mb-exemplars} places
one such tail trajectory beside a representative oscillatory run precisely because the
collapse makes the mechanism legible; the exemplar is the exception, though, and the reader
should hold the freeze---not the bubble---as the modal crash-prone outcome.

\subsection{The Nucleus Is Spatial and Bulk-Born}
\label{sec:mb-spatial}
The survival of the transition told us that budgets do not abolish the crash, but it left
open whether the object that drives it is the same spatial nucleus we anatomized in Model~A
or merely a mean-field instability in which the entire grid tips at once; to decide this we
turned to the seed sweep of Section~\ref{sec:mb-protocols}, running each of the stable,
freeze, and collapse configurations across $20$ disorder seeds, tracking, step by step, the
largest sell cluster as a fraction of the grid, and summarizing each run by the ratio of the
largest cluster to the total sell population at its peak. The three configurations separated
cleanly and without exception. In the stable regime the at-peak ratio sat at a median of
$0.022$ (IQR $0.018$--$0.027$) across every seed---the Sell cells present were scattered into
roughly $500$ dispersed fragments, the largest of which never exceeded about $0.5\%$ of the
grid, and all $20$ trajectories stayed flat---whereas the freeze and collapse regimes
gathered their Sell cells into a single dominant domain, reaching median at-peak ratios of
$0.26$ and $0.44$ respectively with zero overlap against the stable band. That gap was the
signature we sought: below threshold the sell population remained a gas of uncorrelated fluctuations,
while above it one domain nucleated and grew to swallow a macroscopic share of the sell
mass---the crash was a spatial nucleation event in Model~B exactly as it had been in
Model~A, not a synchronous mean-field flip.

The per-seed trajectories made the stochastic character of that nucleation visible. Within
each above-threshold configuration the largest-cluster curves were not scattered noise but a
fan of time-shifted sigmoids---each run climbed from the fluctuation floor to its saturated
domain along the same S-shaped path, but the moment at which it began to climb varied widely
from seed to seed---so that the broad interquartile boxes of the summary panels register
less a spread of outcomes than a spread of nucleation \emph{waiting times}. We read this
dispersion as the Kramers picture surfacing already in Model~B: the wait to nucleate is an escape time whose
realization depends on the particular sequence of contrarian fluctuations a seed happens to
draw, and it was precisely this seed-to-seed jitter in \emph{when} rather than \emph{whether}
a run crossed over that motivated the onset-aligned analyses---front velocities, budget
toggles, and escape-time fits, each clocked from the crash onset $t_{\mathrm{on}}$---that
occupy the remainder of Results~II.

Having established that a nucleus exists, we asked where on the grid it was born---and in
particular whether the active-edge ring that injects the budget-constrained orders is also
the cradle of the winning cluster. The boundary-versus-bulk campaign of
Section~\ref{sec:mb-protocols} tracked the founding cell of the onset cluster across $40$
runs, of which $39$ nucleated within the horizon, and compared its position against the
analytic null for spatially uniform births under which a fraction $0.125$ of foundings fall
within distance~$2$ of the boundary. Births were, if anything, biased away from the edge
rather than toward it: the pooled fraction of onset clusters founded in the boundary band was
$0.077$, statistically indistinguishable from and slightly below the uniform expectation
(binomial $p = 0.47$; the freeze configuration taken alone gave $p = 1.0$); the median
founding cell sat a distance $12$ into the deep bulk; and the typical founding was a
single Sell cell (median birth size $1$ cell, the mark of a single-cell fluctuation), with a
median birth step of $256$ and individual onsets spanning $75$ to $1{,}115$. The winning
nucleus, in short, was overwhelmingly a bulk object---an interior single-cell fluctuation
that happened to survive and grow---rather than a structure templated by the boundary. The
one place the edge did assert itself was \emph{after} birth: pre-onset attachment of the
growing cluster to the active-edge ring occurred only in the collapse configuration ($6$ of
$19$ runs) and never in the freeze ($0$ of $20$), a first hint that the boundary governs how a
nucleus grows rather than where it is born---a distinction we sharpen in
Section~\ref{sec:mb-gating}, when we ask where in the market the budget constraint actually
bites.

\subsection{Budgets Do Not Move the Barrier}
\label{sec:mb-barrier}
A nucleus that is spatial and bulk-born poses the natural next question: does the budget
constraint change how hard such a nucleus is to \emph{create}? Two clean answers suggested
themselves in advance---finite cash might destroy the Kramers scaling altogether, arresting
nucleation so thoroughly that no waiting-time divergence survives, or it might preserve the
scaling while shifting the barrier, sliding the pseudo-critical point upward as budgets
tighten---and the escape-time campaign of Section~\ref{sec:mb-protocols} was built to tell
them apart, sweeping the minority fraction across the onset cliff at three cash levels and
fitting the barrier law $\ln\tilde\tau = A + B/(f - f_0)$, the same form that
Equation~\eqref{eq:kramers} established for Model~A, to the Kaplan--Meier median onset
times at each cash level separately. Neither answer materialized. The escape law survived
at every cash level we probed---the fits achieved $R^2 = 0.978$, $0.990$, and $0.980$ for
cash $500$, $1{,}000$, and $5{,}000$, on $5$, $6$, and $6$ median points respectively,
comparable to the $0.991$ of the Model~A fit---so nucleation under budgets remained what it
had been without them, a stochastic escape over a barrier; and yet the barrier itself, so
far as the data could resolve it, did not move.

Table~\ref{tab:kramers-fits} collects the fitted parameters with their $95\%$ bootstrap
confidence intervals, and Figure~\ref{fig:mb-barrier} draws the fitted divergences over the
medians; together they support the second half of that claim in its precise form---there
was \emph{no detectable barrier shift}. Between the tightest and the loosest budget the
bootstrap intervals overlap on both parameters that encode the barrier: on the
pseudo-critical point $f_0$, cash $500$ gave $(0.000,\,0.153)$ against $(0.077,\,0.157)$ at
cash $5{,}000$; on the barrier coefficient $B$, cash $500$ gave $(0.070,\,1.135)$ against
$(0.050,\,0.415)$; and the onset cliff itself sat between $f = 0.15$ and $f = 0.18$ at
every cash level. We state the accompanying power caveat just as plainly, because an
overlap of intervals is a bound and not a demonstration of equality: the $B$ point
estimates differ by $2.5\times$ across the cash ladder, and the cash-$500$ lower bound of
$0.000$ on $f_0$ is the physical boundary of the fit domain rather than a data
quantile---the data bound the barrier shift, they do not exclude a small one.

The consequence reorganizes the spine of Results~II. Nucleation---the birth of the winning
cluster established in Section~\ref{sec:mb-spatial} and the escape statistics fitted
here---showed no detectable budget dependence: the founding sites were bulk-dominated and
consistent with uniform placement in the tight-cash and loose-cash configurations alike,
and the barrier parameters at cash $500$ could not be told apart from those at cash
$5{,}000$. Whatever budgets do to the crash, then, they must do \emph{downstream} of the
nucleation event, on the growth of the domain the nucleus founds. One exploratory
observation, which we report uncorrected for multiple comparisons and weigh accordingly,
already points the same way: near the cliff the onset rate was monotone in cash---at
$f = 0.174$ the fraction of seeds nucleating within the horizon rose from $0.45$ to $0.62$
to $0.70$ from tight to loose cash, and at $f = 0.177$ the Kaplan--Meier medians fell in
the same order, $1{,}598$, $1{,}427$, and $1{,}208$ steps. Because the onset trigger is
itself a growth milestone---$t_{\mathrm{on}}$ marks the step at which the largest sell
cluster first reaches $5\%$ of the grid---a faster-growing front reaches the trigger
sooner, and a monotone-in-cash onset rate is precisely the signature one expects if budgets
gate growth rather than birth. It is that gating we now set out to demonstrate directly.

\subsection{Growth Is Gated at the Boundary}
\label{sec:mb-gating}
If budgets act on growth rather than birth, two predictions follow---one negative, one
positive---and the campaigns of Section~\ref{sec:mb-protocols} let us test both. The negative
prediction is that handing the interior a ready-made nucleus should buy nothing, because the
interior was never short of nuclei; the interventional arm of the boundary-versus-bulk
campaign therefore stamped a $3 \times 3$ sell patch onto the grid at boundary-adjacent
(edge), mid, and center placements in both the freeze and the collapse configurations, each
stamped run paired against an unstamped control, and the patch failed absolutely as a
founder: in all six cells of the configuration-by-placement design the dominance rate was
$0/20$---the domain standing largest at onset never once descended from the stamped
patch---the patch's forward lineages grew only to $9$--$15$ cells before dying out, never
alive at the moment of onset, and no placement advanced the onset itself. The interior,
evidently, does not lack seeds; the single-cell fluctuations of Section~\ref{sec:mb-spatial}
supply them constantly and at no cost---what is scarce above threshold is not a nucleus but
the \emph{growth} that would make one matter.

Figure~\ref{fig:mb-seeding} lays out the paired onsets, the dominance rates, and exemplar
growth trajectories, and it also contains the one nominal placement effect, which ran, if
anywhere, in the wrong direction for seeding: the freeze-configuration mid-grid patch
\emph{delayed} onset (median paired onset advance $-257$ steps, sign-flip $p = 0.0238$)---an
observation we hold as exploratory, since it is uncorrected across a six-test family, fails
the Bonferroni threshold of $0.0083$, and rests on a sign-flip test of the mean---and we
accordingly report it as a lead, not a result. Even taken at face value, though, it points
the same way as the null it accompanies: a stamped patch could, at best, perturb the interior
into nucleating \emph{later}; nothing we planted made the market crash sooner.

The positive prediction is a dose-response: if suppressed buy orders are the brake on the
front, the front should slow in proportion to how hard the brake is pressed, whatever the
nominal cash endowment that produced the pressing. Across the five budget doses at
$f = 0.20$ (cash $250$--$5{,}000$; $n = 97$ runs included, with $3$ excluded by the window
rule of Section~\ref{sec:mb-protocols}), the front velocity fell as the \emph{absolute}
suppression rate rose---pooled Spearman $\rho = -0.372$, permutation $p = 0.0003$---and the
relationship was not a between-dose confound, for it held within every dose separately, with
$\rho$ between $-0.38$ and $-0.52$, and it survived the removal of any single dose entirely
(leave-one-dose-out $p \le 0.0014$). We state the shape of this result as carefully as its
strength (Figure~\ref{fig:mb-velsupp}). The median suppression load varied remarkably little
across the ladder ($517$--$595$ orders per step), and the median velocity was U-shaped in
cash---highest at the tightest dose, lowest in the middle of the ladder, rising again at the
loosest---so the claim the data support is the pooled, and within-dose, negative
velocity--suppression relationship, not a monotone-in-cash dose ladder: initial cash was a
weak proxy for the suppression a given front actually experienced, and it was the realized
suppression, wherever it landed, that tracked the slowing of the front.

Assembled, the last three subsections close in on where budgets act. Birth is bulk and
free: the winning nucleus is an interior single-cell fluctuation, supplied constantly at
tight and loose budgets alike (Section~\ref{sec:mb-spatial}), and a nine-cell head start planted
anywhere in the interior never founded the winning domain. Escape is budget-blind so far as
the data resolve it: the Kramers law survived at every cash level, and its barrier
parameters could not be told apart between the tightest and the loosest budget
(Section~\ref{sec:mb-barrier}). What budgets throttle is the growth of the domain the
nucleus founds---not whether a nucleus is born, not how long it waits, but how fast it grows
once committed---and the throttle is the suppression of buy orders at the moving front. One
question remains: where in the market that throttle physically sits---through what conduit a
change in cash reaches the front---and it is that conduit we identify causally in
Section~\ref{sec:mb-intervention}.

\subsection{The Intervention Asymmetry}
\label{sec:mb-intervention}
The gating evidence of Section~\ref{sec:mb-gating} still compared runs whose budgets
differed from the first step; the budget toggle of Section~\ref{sec:mb-protocols} sharpened
that comparison into a true intervention: for each seed we ran paired counterfactuals---one
control, one in which every cell's cash was scaled by a common factor exactly at
$t_{\mathrm{on}}$, the pre-toggle histories asserted bit-identical---and we read the
consequences in the onset-aligned window of $W = 400$ steps, so that whatever divergence a
pair developed dated strictly from the liquidity shock. The injection arm delivered the
causal answer, and in the direction the gating picture predicts: scaling cash $\times 10$
at onset in the tight freeze configuration (cash $500$) \emph{accelerated} the front in
$14$ of $19$ seeds (sign test $p = 0.064$; we report the count and the $p$-value as they
stand)---the median front velocity rose from $1.39$ to $3.55 \times 10^{-4}$ grid fractions
per step, the median front extent at $t_{\mathrm{on}}+400$ from $0.112$ to $0.192$, and the
suppressed buy orders fell by $29\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:mb-toggle}; the window rule of
Section~\ref{sec:mb-protocols} admitted $19$ of the $20$ seeds in every arm). Handing the
market ten times its cash at the moment of crash onset made the sentiment crash spread
\emph{faster}; it is the paired design, not the seed count, that licenses the causal
reading, and it is the mechanism traced below that explains it.

An acceleration under injection is exactly backwards for the naive mechanism, and that is
what makes it diagnostic. Injected cash cannot feed the front directly---the front is a
\emph{sell} wave, sells need shares rather than cash, and the interior CA never sees the
books at all, since budgets suppress order flow and never sentiment
(Section~\ref{sec:model-b})---so the only conduit by which a cash shock can reach interior
sentiment is the loop's own: executed flow moves the price, the price crosses the edge
bands, and the band crossings set the state of the active-edge ring whose sentiment the
boundary feeds into the grid. The edge trace of Figure~\ref{fig:mb-edgetrace} caught that
conduit in the act. At the toggle, injection flipped the ring \emph{instantly}, from the
control's ${\sim}0.5$ Buy and $0$ Sell to $0$ Buy and ${\sim}1.0$ Sell: the injected cash
let the buy orders the books had been suppressing execute at last, the supported
price---$113$ against the control's $93$ at $t_{\mathrm{on}}+400$---broke above the upper
bands, and the entire ring, contrarian in the value trader's sense of buying below its band
and selling above it, dumped at once, the flipped state decaying back over the following
$100$--$200$ steps as the EMA bands caught up with the new price level. Under tight budgets
the boundary bought into the falling price and braked the front; under injection it sold into
the supported price and fed the front instead. The boundary switched from brake to
accelerant---a sign reversal, not a modulation.

Withdrawal, which by symmetry ought to have braked the crash, did nothing of the
kind---at either dose, and the two doses matter because together they rule out mere
under-dosing. The mild cut ($\times 0.1$, applied in the loose collapse configuration,
cash $5{,}000$) left the front statistically untouched (sign-flip permutation $p = 0.67$)
and barely disturbed the suppression load, suppressed buys moving $+7\%$; the harsh cut
($\times 0.01$) \emph{binds}---it dropped every cell to ${\sim}50$ in cash, below the
standing price, so buys died outright and the suppressed-buy load jumped from $524$ to
$680$ orders per step---and yet the median front velocity moved only from $2.23$ to
$2.38 \times 10^{-4}$ grid fractions per step ($11$ of $19$ seeds in the predicted
direction; sign-flip permutation $p = 0.29$), while the price barely registered the cut at
all, $119.5$ against the control's $123.6$ at $t_{\mathrm{on}}+400$. The trace explains
the null (Figure~\ref{fig:mb-edgetrace}): in the loose configuration the control ring
already sat near $0.46$ Buy after onset---a brake the crash itself had engaged, the sell
wave having pressed the price beneath nearly half the ring's bands---and the cash cut
could only strengthen it transiently, lifting the ring's Buy fraction to
${\sim}0.72$--$0.74$ before it relaxed back to the control level within ${\sim}200$
steps: the same sign, a modest margin, and a decay.

Why the asymmetry? Two features of the model's economy combine to produce it. The first is
a matter of sign and ceiling: only injection can \emph{reverse} the boundary's state,
because propping the price above the upper bands flips the ring from buying to selling,
whereas withdrawal can move the ring only \emph{further} in the direction it already
points, and only transiently---an engaged brake pressed harder, not a new force. The
second is that crashes \emph{self-finance} their own stabilizers: every cell the front
recruits turns seller and liquidates up to its $10$-share endowment at prices near
$P \approx 100$--$120$, banking on the order of $1{,}000$ in fresh cash per recruit, so
the sell wave continuously mints the liquidity its dip-buyers spend, and a cut to the cash
\emph{stock} at one instant cannot staunch the cash \emph{flow} the crash generates for
itself. Together they yield the phenomenology we take from Model~B as its headline applied
result---a \emph{liquidity-intervention asymmetry}, in which injections prop the price,
flip the contrarian boundary to selling, and accelerate the sentiment crash, while
withdrawals are near-inert. What this asymmetry means for the design of liquidity
interventions, and for the intuition that support operations are stabilizing, we take up
in the Discussion.

% ============================================================================
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================================

The intervention asymmetry of Section~\ref{sec:mb-intervention} is the result we most
want to hold against the crisis literature, because it inverts the intuition that
support operations are stabilizing: in the model, liquidity support did not merely fail
to arrest the sentiment crash---it recruited the market's own stabilizers into
accelerating it. The injected cash let the suppressed buys execute, the supported price
broke above the contrarian bands, and the boundary ring that had been buying into the
falling price turned to selling into the supported one---the front accelerated in $14$
of $19$ paired seeds (sign test $p = 0.064$, reported as it stands; it is the paired
design that licenses the causal reading)---while withdrawal was null at both
doses, even the harsh cut that demonstrably bound the books, because a crash
\emph{self-finances} its own dip-buyers: every cell the front recruits turns seller and
mints the cash the next dip-buyer spends, so a one-time cut to the cash stock cannot
staunch the flow the crash generates for itself. The acceleration has an empirical
shadow in the Flash Crash record of Kirilenko et al.~\cite{kirilenko2017flash}, in
which the high-frequency intermediaries who might have absorbed a large sell order
instead amplified it; and the underlying entanglement---funding conditions and market
stability reinforcing one another rather than damping---is the liquidity spiral that
Brunnermeier and Pedersen~\cite{brunnermeier2009market} formalize, though our loop runs
the spiral with the sign reversed, support feeding the crash rather than funding shocks
feeding illiquidity. The kinship with the collective moral hazard of Farhi and
Tirole~\cite{farhi2012collective} is the more instructive for being partial: in their
account support destabilizes \emph{prospectively}, the anticipation of a bailout
inducing the correlated risk-taking that later makes the bailout necessary, whereas in
our model support destabilizes \emph{contemporaneously}, through no expectational
channel at all---the agents anticipate nothing---but through the mechanical conduit of
price to band to boundary state. We attach the caveat in the same breath as the claim:
that conduit runs through a stylized contrarian band rule on a lattice boundary,
calibrated to nothing, so the analogy we assert is structural---liquidity support can
strengthen the very feedback it is deployed to break---and nothing more.

The staging of the crash---birth, escape, growth---also locates where an intervention
can expect leverage. Nucleation showed no detectable budget dependence: the winning
nucleus was born in the bulk, consistent with uniform placement at tight and loose cash
alike (Section~\ref{sec:mb-spatial}), and the fitted barrier parameters could not be
told apart across a tenfold cash ladder (Section~\ref{sec:mb-barrier}); growth, by
contrast, was gated---throttled by suppressed buy orders at the moving front and
reached causally through the price-to-boundary conduit (Sections~\ref{sec:mb-gating}
and~\ref{sec:mb-intervention}). If that division of labor generalizes, the lesson is
one of stage selection. An intervention on aggregate liquidity addresses the wrong
stage: it cannot touch birth, because interior fluctuations supply nuclei constantly
and at no cost, and what it reaches instead is growth, through a conduit whose sign the
intervener does not control. An intervention that acts directly on order flow at the
growing interface addresses the stage the model identifies as controllable---the
category to which circuit-breaker-style arrest belongs, with the caution of
Subrahmanyam~\cite{subrahmanyam1994circuit} attached, that anticipation of the halt can
accelerate the very trading it is meant to stop and raise volatility rather than curb
it, so that targeting the right stage does not by itself guarantee the right sign. We
offer this as a model-generated hypothesis about the leverage points of a spreading
crash---birth is cheap and everywhere, growth is gated and local---and emphatically not
as policy advice: the economy in which it holds is a lattice of one-share orders filled
against an infinite market maker, and the hypothesis earns no more authority than that
economy can lend it.

The limitations of the study bound how far these readings travel. Model~A's empirical
validation rests on a single price series---the QQQ ETF over the 2024 calendar
year---so robustness across instruments and market regimes remains to be established;
the Model~B nucleation campaigns all ran on a single $64 \times 64$ grid trading a
single asset against an infinite-inventory market maker, and while the weak system-size
dependence of the Model~A transition makes grid-size robustness plausible, the budget
results have not been rechecked at other scales and the model is silent on cross-asset
contagion. The agents themselves are deterministic---the CA rules incorporate no
stochastic noise, no learning, and no adaptive strategy switching, features of real
markets whose inclusion would enrich the dynamics at the cost of breaking the
enumerability of the rule space. On the theory side, the analytical hierarchy of
Section~\ref{sec:theory} treats the contrarian disorder as annealed, averaging over
rule types in the mean-field and pair maps, whereas the simulations fix each
contrarian's placement for the duration of a run---quenched disorder in the proper
sense---and a quenched large-deviation calculation would sharpen the prediction of
$f_0$ and connect the critical nucleus size to the combinatorics of contrarian
clustering. And the statistics of Results~II carry flags that interpretation must not
launder: the monotone-in-cash onset rate near the cliff (Section~\ref{sec:mb-barrier})
and the freeze mid-patch onset delay (Section~\ref{sec:mb-gating}) are exploratory,
uncorrected observations; the injection effect stands at $14$ of $19$ seeds with a
sign-test $p = 0.064$, and it is the paired design, not the count, that carries the
causal reading; and the barrier-shift null is a power-bounded statement---the
overlapping confidence intervals bound a shift, they do not exclude a small one.

A last question shadows any two-rule study: how much hangs on the two rules? The
totalistic framework at least makes the question answerable, in a way unavailable to
models with continuous behavioral parameters, because the rule space is finite and
enumerable---$3^9 = 19{,}683$ totalistic rules---and the majority/contrarian pair
studied here, two rules that differ at a single neighbourhood sum, is one point in that
space. Whether the nucleation spine assembled in this paper---metastable attractor,
disorder-seeded nucleus, budget-gated growth---recurs across other rule pairings, and
whether the intervention asymmetry survives boundary rules other than the contrarian
band, are questions a systematic screen of the rule space can settle; that screen is
future work.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================================

We presented a minimal agent-based model of financial markets in which traders on a
two-dimensional grid follow deterministic Wolfram totalistic cellular automaton rules.
The model required only two ingredients---a majority/herding rule (19280) and a
contrarian/destabilizer rule (19286)---and a single control parameter, the minority
agent fraction~$f$. We observed a sharp first-order phase transition at
$f_c \approx 0.15$: below this threshold the market persisted in a metastable
mixed-state attractor; above it, the system collapsed to an all-Sell absorbing state
with a discontinuous drop in the Potts energy ($\Delta e \approx 0.56$) and the
site entropy ($\Delta S_1 \approx 0.88$).

The principal theoretical contribution of this work is the identification and
characterisation of the transition mechanism through a hierarchy of successively
refined approximations. The mean-field self-consistency map
(Section~\ref{sec:mean-field}) predicted no transition: the buy-dominated fixed point
remained linearly stable for all $f \in [0,1]$. The pair approximation
(Section~\ref{sec:pair-approx}), which incorporates nearest-neighbour correlations
via a synchronous pair-density map, likewise found no instability---the spectral
radius of the Jacobian at the uncorrelated fixed point was strictly below unity for
all~$f$, and the spatial mutual information decayed to zero from every tested initial
condition. The failure of \emph{both} approximations establishes by elimination that
the transition is a \emph{nucleation} phenomenon: the system crosses the barrier
between the metastable mixed attractor and the all-Sell ground state via a rare,
large-scale fluctuation that no finite-order correlation expansion can capture.

The nucleation theory (Section~\ref{sec:nucleation}) quantified this mechanism
directly. The metastable lifetime followed the Kramers escape scaling
$\ln \bar{\tau} = a + B/(f - f_0)$ with $R^2 = 0.991$, identifying a critical
disorder threshold $f_0 \approx 0.10$ below which the nucleation barrier is infinite
and the mixed state is absolutely stable. The observed $f_c \approx 0.15$ is not a
sharp thermodynamic phase boundary but an observation-time-dependent crossover. The
mechanism is disorder-driven: each contrarian agent creates a point-like sell excess
(influence radius ${\sim}1$~lattice spacing), and nucleation occurs when random
clustering of contrarians produces a local density sufficient to seed a sell domain
that exceeds the critical size; the majority rule then amplifies this seed into a
coherent wavefront.

Three additional features distinguished this framework. First, the Potts energy
mapping (Section~\ref{sec:potts}) connects the CA dynamics to zero-temperature
three-state Potts model physics, with the contrarian fraction~$f$ playing the role
of quenched disorder strength, grounding the model in established statistical
mechanics. Second, the transition was discontinuous, placing it within the
tipping-point framework of Scheffer et al.~\cite{scheffer2009early}; the Kramers
scaling of the metastable lifetime suggests that early-warning signals (rising
variance and autocorrelation of the sell fraction) may be detectable in the
pre-nucleation regime. Third, the full rule space is finite and enumerable
($3^9 = 19{,}683$ rules), enabling systematic screening of agent
behaviours---a property unavailable in models with continuous behavioural parameters.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The present study uses a single price series (QQQ, 2024 calendar year) for Model~A
validation; robustness across instruments and market regimes remains to be established.
The CA rules are deterministic and do not incorporate stochastic noise, learning, or
adaptive strategy switching---features that are present in real markets and that would
enrich the model at the cost of breaking the enumerability of the rule space. The
nucleation theory treats the contrarian disorder as annealed (averaging over rule
types) rather than quenched (fixed spatial assignment); a quenched large-deviation
calculation would sharpen the prediction of~$f_0$ and connect the critical nucleus
size to the combinatorics of contrarian clustering.

\paragraph{Future directions.}
Model~B (closed-loop endogenous price) remains to be fully characterised; its
four-dimensional parameter space $(k, w, \lambda, f)$ may admit a stability analysis
that yields a closed-form expression for the phase boundary. A key open question is
whether Model~B self-organises to criticality without fine-tuning of~$f$---i.e.,
whether the reflexive feedback loop drives the system toward its own tipping point,
a phenomenon that would connect this framework to the broader literature on
self-organised criticality in financial
markets~\cite{sornette2017why,bak1987selforganized,gabaix2003theory}. On the
theoretical side, a quenched-disorder nucleation calculation---using
large-deviation statistics of the local contrarian density to predict the critical
nucleus size and the barrier height $\Delta G^*(f)$ from first principles---would
close the gap between the phenomenological Kramers fit and a fully predictive theory.
