\begin{abstract}
Changes in investor decisions can spread through a market, but the
resulting price decline depends on how trades execute. We developed a
market model in which traders inside a grid decided whether to buy,
hold, or sell from their neighbors' decisions, while technical traders
along the edge of the grid responded to price. Paired markets shared the
same history until selected traders in one market received a lasting
change in their decision rule, while a no-switch control kept the
original rule. A Sell majority formed once a large enough fraction of
traders received the new rule, although every other trader kept the
original one. When the technical traders were kept in Hold so that no
decision responded to the price, majorities still formed, so responses
to neighbors alone could produce widespread selling. The probability of
a majority rose over a narrow range of that fraction, a crossover that
moved to smaller fractions in larger markets and over longer observation
periods rather than marking a fixed threshold. To connect decisions to
prices, we cleared orders against a dealer whose finite cash and share
reserves kept a constant product and set the quoted price. Shallow
positive reserves allowed severe declines while rejecting few orders,
and such markets also fell sharply without the rule switch, so the
switch's effect was measured against the paired controls. Changing
trader cash and shares altered later decisions only when technical
traders could respond to the resulting prices, and transaction fees
reduced price declines in some conditions but deepened them in others.
When some traders instead followed random advice from one shared source,
their influence on neighboring traders spread selling and, with deep
dealer reserves, let them gain cash at the other traders' expense. The
model thus separated the spread of selling decisions from the severity
of a simulated crash.
\end{abstract}
