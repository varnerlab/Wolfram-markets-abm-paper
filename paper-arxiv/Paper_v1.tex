\begin{abstract}
Publicly expressed trading intentions can influence others even when no
transaction occurs. We developed a market model in which traders on a
grid responded to neighbors' intentions without observing their holdings,
while technical traders responded to price. Paired markets shared a
history before selected traders in one market received a lasting change
in their decision rule. Under the original observation rule, a Sell
majority formed once enough traders received the new rule, even when
technical traders were kept in Hold and no decision responded to price.
Majority probability rose over a narrow range of switched fractions that
moved with market size, observation time, and revision frequency. Orders
cleared against a finite constant-product dealer. Shallow positive
reserves allowed severe declines even without a rule switch, so its
effect was measured against paired controls. Giving traders fewer shares
reduced declines, while transaction fees reduced them in some conditions
and deepened them in others. In a hypothetical information regime,
traders read Hold when a neighbor intended to sell but had no shares.
This change reduced the switch's effect on late selling intentions and
drawdown in the primary comparison, while majorities reappeared at higher
switched fractions. In separate experiments, traders following one shared
source of random advice spread selling and, with deep dealer reserves,
gained cash at other traders' expense. The model connected the spread of
public intentions to their financial consequences and showed how those
consequences depended on the observation and execution rules.
\end{abstract}
