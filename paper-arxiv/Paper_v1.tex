\begin{abstract}
Changes in investor decisions can destabilize a market, but a shift toward
selling need not produce a price crash. We developed a market model in which
traders inside a grid decided whether to buy, hold, or sell from their
neighbors' decisions, while technical traders along its edge decided from
the price alone. We compared markets with identical
histories, then changed how selected traders in one market responded to
their neighbors while leaving the comparison market unchanged. A majority
decided to sell when enough traders received the new rule, even though the
remaining traders kept their original rules. Setting the selected traders
to Sell once without changing their rule for later decisions did not produce a
majority, while several other changes to a single response did. A majority
still formed when the technical traders were kept in Hold, showing that
responses to neighboring traders could sustain widespread selling on their
own. Markets with more interacting traders shifted toward a
majority over a narrower range of rule adoption, but the fraction needed
continued to change with the time observed. The dealer's capacity to buy and
sell affected how often a majority formed when the technical traders were
active, although greater capacity did not consistently make a majority more likely.
Greater capacity did increase price declines in the comparison where a majority formed at every dealer capacity, even when we changed how strongly trades affected prices. Varying
cash and share holdings explained this difference: traders needed resources
to place orders, and the dealer needed resources to complete orders that
other traders could not match. Similar Sell majorities could therefore
accompany both small price declines and simulated crashes. The model
connected investor decisions to market instability while showing how limited
dealer resources could contain the price decline even when most traders
continued deciding to sell.
\end{abstract}
