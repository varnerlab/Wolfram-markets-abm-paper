We next asked whether traders who followed a common source of advice,
rather than their neighbors, spread their decisions to other traders and
gained or lost cash as a result. In these markets no trader received a
rule switch, and the advice was a random sequence unrelated to price, so
a gain could not come from information about the asset. When 40\% of
interior traders followed one shared source at dealer depth $h=100$ with
technical traders held, non-followers spent 19.12 percentage points more
of their time in Sell than the same traders in the no-following
controls, where they spent about a quarter of their time in Sell
(Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:following}A). The followers ended with
26.71 percentage points more cash, relative to their value when
following began, than the same traders without following, with a
Bonferroni-adjusted 97.5\% interval of [25.43, 28.06]. When non-followers
instead read each follower's intention from the no-following control,
their decisions were unchanged and the followers' gain fell to 2.89
percentage points, so removing the followers' influence on neighboring
traders lowered the gain by 23.82 percentage points, with an adjusted
interval of [22.30, 25.30]. The
gain was a transfer rather than new wealth: after every trader sold its
shares, the dealer's cash and the traders' total cash matched the
no-following control, so the followers gained what the other traders
lost. With shallow dealer reserves the followers lost instead, by 3.12
percentage points at dealer depth $h=1$ with technical traders held and
by 18.08 with them active. Following also deepened price declines: with
technical traders held, the maximum drawdown rose over the controls by
27.53 percentage points at dealer depth $h=100$ and by 55.35 at $h=1$.
When the advice stopped after 1,000 steps, the followers kept a gain of
12.19 percentage points. Non-followers kept selling in some markets but
not in others: in 38 of 100 runs they still spent more than 20
percentage points more of the final 500 steps in Sell, whereas in 61 of
100 the increase was below 5 percentage points (Supplementary
Table~\ref{tab:following}B). Separate
advice for each follower lowered non-follower selling by 13.00
percentage points and gave the followers a gain of only 4.87. Thus,
shared advice spread through neighboring decisions, and followers gained
cash at other traders' expense when the dealer had deep reserves mostly
because their intentions influenced their neighbors, not because the
advice carried information.

In an exploratory comparison over 10 other starting markets, we also
varied the number of advice sources and compared advice with copying
another trader's completed trades, with 40\% followers at dealer depth
$h=100$ and technical traders held (Supplementary
Table~\ref{tab:following}C). Replacing one source with sixteen changed
non-follower selling from an increase of 18.90 percentage points to a
decrease of 10.92 and lowered the followers' gain from 27.01 to 6.13
percentage points. Copying changed the followers' cash far less than
shared advice did, by 0.58 percentage points when every follower copied
one trader and 0.81 when groups copied sixteen. Copying sixteen traders
lowered non-follower selling by 18.31 percentage points. Copying one
trader gave a mean change of 4.07 percentage points with an interval
from $-6.56$ to 16.03, because non-follower selling rose in half of the
runs and fell in the other half. Thus, agreement among followers shaped
their effect on other traders: one shared source spread
selling and brought the largest gains, whereas many sources, separate
advice, or copying many traders reduced non-follower selling. These
exploratory comparisons were not repeated on new starting markets.
