\section{Conclusion}

A lasting change in how a fraction of traders responded to their
neighbors could move a market from mixed decisions to a Sell majority,
even when no trader's decision responded to the price. The fraction
needed fell in larger markets and when the market was observed for
longer, and rose when traders revised their decisions less often, so it
marked a crossover for finite markets, observation periods, and revision
timing rather than a fixed threshold. A Sell majority did not by itself
determine how far the price fell. Trader cash and shares limited which
orders could be completed, and the dealer's cash and share reserves set
the prices at which the remaining orders cleared. Shallow positive
dealer reserves allowed severe declines with or without the rule switch,
whereas zero dealer depth kept the price fixed because the dealer did
not trade. Small dealer reserves therefore did not protect against a
crash, although giving traders fewer shares reduced the decline. Traders
who followed one
shared source of random advice spread selling through their neighbors
and, when the dealer's reserves were deep, gained cash that the other
traders lost. The financial
consequence of widespread selling depended on how orders were executed
and priced, not only on how many traders intended to sell.
