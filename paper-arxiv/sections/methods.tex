\section{Methods}

\subsection{Three-state cellular automaton}

We represented investors as traders on an $L\times L$ square grid,
called a lattice. Traders inside the grid decided whether to buy, hold,
or sell from their immediate neighbors' decisions. Selected traders along the edge used changes in price instead; keeping these edge traders in Hold
allowed us to remove the influence of price on decisions. Buy ($s=0$), Hold ($s=1$), and Sell
($s=2$) represented the resulting decisions, and the local rule specified
which decision a trader made for a given neighbor configuration. Only the $(L-2)^2$ interior
cells followed the cellular-automaton rule. All interior traders updated together from their four nearest
neighbors, denoted by $\partial i$. Adding the numbered decisions of
these neighbors gave the sum $c_i(t)$. A lookup table ${\cal R}$ assigned
one next decision $a_j={\cal R}(j)$ to each possible sum. A rule based
only on this sum is called totalistic.
The sum ranks Buy below Hold and Sell but does not distinguish all
combinations of neighboring decisions. Two Holds and two
Buys receive the same response as one Sell and three Buys.
Changing one table entry therefore altered the response to a specified
neighbor sum while preserving the other eight responses. Neighborhoods
with the same sum remained equivalent under the altered rule.
The local input and the Wolfram index $R$ encoding the nine
outputs were defined by the expressions~\cite{wolfram1984universality,packard1985two}:
\begin{gather}
  c_i(t)=\sum_{j\in\partial i}s_j(t)\in\{0,1,\ldots,8\},
  \label{eq:neighborhood-sum}\\
  R=\sum_{j=0}^{8}a_j3^j.
  \label{eq:rule-index}
\end{gather}

Rule~19280, $(222110002)_3$, supplied the baseline decision rule.
The activated Rule~19286, $(222110022)_3$, differed only at $c=1$.
A neighborhood containing three Buys and one Hold therefore returned Buy
under the baseline and Sell under activation. Before each run, we selected
a fraction $f$ of interior sites uniformly without replacement as the target
set for the later switch. The initial interior contained
$\operatorname{round}(0.05(L-2)^2)$ Buy sites and the same number of Sell
sites, with Hold elsewhere. Initial states were placed without replacement.
A random-number seed identified each starting market and fixed the
initial placements, selected traders, active edge traders, and their
price-response parameters. It also fixed the priorities used
when eligible orders exceeded dealer capacity. Once these choices were
made, the market evolved deterministically. Delaying activation allowed
the market to develop under the baseline rule before the response changed.
At the intervention, each targeted trader retained its current decision
and holdings but used the new rule to make subsequent decisions. Traders
outside the target set kept their original decision rule throughout.

\subsection{Orders, trader resources, and completed trades}

A trader's decision generated a desired order, but carrying out that
decision required sufficient cash or shares and a counterparty willing
to take the other side. Every interior cell and
active edge cell represented a trader with cash $w_i$, an integer
number of shares $q_i$, and average purchase price $\bar c_i$.
Buy requested the purchase of one share, Sell requested the sale of one
share, and Hold requested no trade. An order was eligible, meaning backed by the required trader resources,
if it could be paid for or supplied at the current price $P_{t-1}$. A buy required
$w_i\geq P_{t-1}$, while a sell required $q_i\geq1$. The main experiments
disabled the optional cost-basis restriction, which permits sales only above
average acquisition cost in the manner of the disposition
effect~\cite{shefrin1985disposition,odean1998investors}.
We excluded orders lacking the required cash or shares and recorded
this exclusion as \emph{agent suppression}. The trader's decision
remained unchanged even though its order could not complete.
A trader could therefore remain in Sell after exhausting its shares,
or in Buy without enough cash to purchase a share. Its neighbors still
responded to that state during the lattice update. We then matched the remaining orders with other traders or the dealer
to determine which trades could complete
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:dealer-clearing}).

We first matched buy orders with sell orders from other traders. With $B_t$
eligible buys and $S_t$ eligible sells, $\min(B_t,S_t)$ units on each side
executed without using dealer resources. The dealer bought the shares that other traders did not buy, or sold
the shares that other traders did not supply, within its cash and share
limits. Capacity $h\geq0$ specified
net units per trader, giving total capacity
$H=\operatorname{round}(hN_{\rm tr})$ for $N_{\rm tr}$ traders. A finite
dealer began with cash $W_0=P_0H$ and inventory $Q_0=H$. It could not borrow
and had to satisfy $0\leq Q_t\leq2H$. When buys exceeded sells, executed
counts were:
\begin{align}
 n_t^S &= S_t, &
 n_t^B &= S_t+\min(B_t-S_t,Q_{t-1}).
 \label{eq:dealer-buy-excess}
\end{align}
When sells exceeded buys, execution also depended on the dealer's remaining
inventory space and ability to pay for shares:
\begin{align}
 n_t^B &= B_t, &
 n_t^S &= B_t+\min\!\left(S_t-B_t,2H-Q_{t-1},
                 \left\lfloor\frac{W_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}}\right\rfloor\right).
 \label{eq:dealer-sell-excess}
\end{align}
Eligible orders beyond these limits were \emph{dealer-rejected}.
When the dealer could not complete every remaining order, a
reproducible priority score determined which traders could trade. The
score depended on the seed, step, trader identity, and whether the order
was a buy or sell. Including the step allowed priority to change over
time while keeping it reproducible across paired runs. Completing trades at $P_{t-1}$ updated trader holdings and changed
dealer cash and shares by the corresponding amounts:
\begin{align}
  Q_t &= Q_{t-1}+n_t^S-n_t^B, &
  W_t &= W_{t-1}+P_{t-1}(n_t^B-n_t^S).
  \label{eq:dealer-ledger}
\end{align}
These transfers conserved total cash and shares across traders and dealer,
except when we intentionally changed trader cash or shares. The unlimited dealer, $h=\infty$, started with zero cash and shares
but could borrow cash and sell borrowed shares to fill every eligible
order. At $h=0$, only opposing trader orders
could clear. Both limits used the same clearing procedure
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:dealer-clearing}).

\subsection{How price changes influenced trader decisions}

Traders along the edge of the grid brought price information into
neighboring traders' decisions. These edge traders compared the current
price with recent prices, while the interior traders used their
neighbors' decisions. The difference between completed trader buys and
sells determined the price change. We normalized the difference between filled buys and sells
by the fixed trader count $N_{\rm tr}$ to obtain imbalance $\phi_t$.
Positive imbalance raised the price, negative imbalance lowered it,
and balanced execution left it unchanged. The exponential update used
price-impact coefficient $\lambda=0.02$ and initial price $P_0=100$.
Active edge traders compared each new price with a reference price
$\mu_t$, updated from recent prices, and a measure $\sigma_t$ of recent
price departures from that reference. Each trader had a
fixed multiplier $k_j\sim U[k(1-\delta),k(1+\delta)]$, with $k=1$
and $\delta=0.5$. The price range in which this trader chose Hold extended from
$\mu_t-k_j\sigma_t$ to $\mu_t+k_j\sigma_t$. The trader chose Buy below this range, Sell above it, and Hold inside it. One quarter of
perimeter cells were active; the others stayed at Hold.
These decisions influenced adjacent interior traders, allowing price
changes from completed trades to affect later decisions inside the grid.
Imbalance, price, and the reference price and measure of recent price variation followed
these updates, with smoothing parameter $\alpha=1/60$:
\begin{gather}
  \phi_t=\frac{n_t^B-n_t^S}{N_{\rm tr}},
  \label{eq:imbalance}\\
  P_t=P_{t-1}\exp(\lambda\phi_t),
  \label{eq:price-update}\\
  \mu_t=\mu_{t-1}+\alpha(P_t-\mu_{t-1}),\qquad
  \sigma_t=\sigma_{t-1}+\alpha(|P_t-\mu_t|-\sigma_{t-1}).
  \label{eq:adaptive-band}
\end{gather}

Orders came from the current trader states, while the resulting price
influenced the next interior update
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:model-step}).
At each step, rule activation and any financial intervention preceded
eligibility tests, clearing, and settlement. Executed orders then generated
the new price. Boundary traders responded to the price before the interior cells updated
synchronously. The complete step followed the sequence:
\begin{equation*}
 \text{lattice state}\rightarrow\text{orders}\rightarrow\text{dealer fills}
 \rightarrow P_t\rightarrow\text{boundary state}\rightarrow\text{lattice state}.
\end{equation*}
To prevent price changes from affecting decisions, we set every active
edge trader to Hold before each interior update. We call this condition
\emph{held boundary}; \emph{endogenous boundary} denotes the condition
in which edge traders responded to the price generated by trades.
The comparison kept the same initial grid, selected traders, active
edge locations, price-response parameters, trader holdings, and dealer. Orders still passed through the
same eligibility, clearing, and price rules.
Cash, shares, and price could still change, but these changes could no
longer affect decisions inside the grid. Interior traders responded
only to neighboring decisions, allowing us to determine whether a Sell
majority could form without information about price changes (Algorithms~\ref{alg:model-step} and~\ref{alg:dealer-clearing}).

\subsection{Paired rule-switch experiments}

We compared the consequences of changing a decision rule with continued
use of the original rule in paired markets that shared a common history. Both paths used Rule~19280 at every interior site for
$T_{\rm pre}=1{,}000$ baseline steps. At the start of step
$T_{\rm pre}+1$, one path activated Rule~19286 at the preselected fraction
$f$. The matched control left those sites on Rule~19280.
Neither path reset the lattice, price, edge traders' price-response ranges, trader holdings, or
dealer. Their common state $X_{T_{\rm pre}}$ also generated the first price
after activation, so the earliest possible difference occurred in the
following interior update. We checked the shared pre-switch histories and
first post-switch price bit for bit in every pair.
Each switched path thus began with the same trader resources and
neighbor configurations as its control. The four main sets of simulations used 50 seeds and 3,000 steps after
the rule change, with initial trader cash of 1,000 and ten shares each. Additional comparisons tested other rules, a one-time instruction to
sell, fixed widths for the edge traders' Hold ranges, different strengths
of the price response to trades, and longer observation periods.
The supplementary protocols specify which part of the intervention
or feedback was changed in each control (Appendix~\ref{app:revision-controls}).

We varied the fraction of traders given the new rule, grid size, and
financial resources to determine when a Sell majority formed and how
many sales could complete (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:campaigns}).
The selected switch
experiment used $L=64$, an unlimited dealer, and an endogenous boundary at
four activation fractions. We then tested more fractions at eight dealer capacities, both with
edge traders responding to price and with those traders kept in Hold. The finite-size campaign
used $L\in\{32,48,64,96,128\}$ under a held boundary with $h=\infty$ and
endogenous boundaries with $h=1$ or $\infty$. Because capacity was defined
per trader, absolute dealer resources increased with lattice size.
The financial campaign multiplied current trader cash and shares independently
by $g_w,g_q\in\{0.1,1,10\}$ at activation, rounding shares to integers.
Its nine interventions shared the same pre-intervention path within each
$(f,h,\text{boundary},\text{seed})$ cell. Under the held boundary, they also
had to retain identical lattice trajectories afterward because neither cash nor shares entered the
interior update directly.
A discrepancy would therefore indicate an implementation error.
The appendix gives the full parameter grids and the checks used to verify
these paired and held-boundary relationships.

\subsection{Market outcomes, statistical analysis, and selection of examples}

We used the strictly interior Sell fraction $m(t)$ to measure the
shift toward selling. Larger values indicated that more interior
traders occupied Sell, and we treated the emergence of a Sell majority
after the rule change as a sign of market instability.
We recorded a Sell majority when $m(t)\geq0.5$ at least once after the
rule change. The onset time was the first step that reached this value,
and majority probability, also called transition probability, was the
fraction of runs that reached it. We summarized onset times only for
runs that reached a majority and averaged the Sell fraction over the
final 500 steps to describe each market near the end of the observation
period.
To measure how much this final mean $\bar m$ differed between runs,
we multiplied its variance by the number of interior traders,
$(L-2)^2$. Angle brackets denote averages across seeds. The resulting
quantity, called susceptibility $\chi_L$, is used to compare systems
of different sizes~\cite{binder1981finite,landau2021guide}:
\begin{equation}
 \chi_L=(L-2)^2\left[\langle\bar m^2\rangle-
                         \langle\bar m\rangle^2\right].
 \label{eq:susceptibility}
\end{equation}
At a fixed grid size, higher susceptibility indicated greater
variation in the final mean Sell fraction between starting markets. Linear
interpolation gave $f_q(L)$, the activation fraction at which the
transition probability reached $q/100$. The midpoint was $f_{50}(L)$,
and the width $f_{75}(L)-f_{25}(L)$ measured how much additional
activation separated probabilities of 0.25 and 0.75. A smaller width
therefore indicated a sharper response.
Interpolation used the first upward crossing without forcing the sampled
curve to be monotone. Binomial proportions used 95\% Wilson
intervals~\cite{wilson1927probable}; continuous summaries used interquartile
ranges unless otherwise specified.

A high Sell fraction need not produce a large price decline if the
requested orders cannot execute. We measured the decline by maximum
drawdown, the largest proportional fall from a running price peak
during the post-activation window. We used price crash to describe a
large simulated decline and reported its magnitude through drawdown,
without defining a cutoff for classifying runs as crashes. We recorded unfilled orders at the
stage where resources prevented execution. Agent suppression counted
desired orders excluded by insufficient trader cash or shares.
Dealer rejection counted eligible orders left unfilled after matching
because the dealer lacked cash or inventory capacity. In the production campaigns, the reported rejection fraction used
all eligible orders over the full run, including the baseline steps,
as its denominator, whereas the supplementary controls used
post-activation orders only. Higher rejection fractions meant the dealer prevented more orders
backed by trader resources from completing. Financial summaries used medians
and interquartile ranges across seeds. Drawdown measured the price decline caused by completed trades, while
suppression and rejection identified why desired trades could not
complete.

Testing too few fractions of traders with the new rule could miss the
range over which majority probability changed. We refined each curve using a common
resolution criterion. Intervals intersecting the empirical 5--95\% response were
bisected until every size and boundary/capacity condition had at least five
fractions strictly inside that probability range. Each added fraction used
the original 50 seeds. We then resampled whole seeds across all fractions
2,000 times to estimate uncertainty in the crossings, width, and sampled
susceptibility maximum. These percentile intervals preserved dependence
between fractions and included reselection of the susceptibility peak.
We also fitted a logistic curve, which required majority probability
$p_L(f)$ to increase smoothly with the fraction $f$. The fitted
parameters $a_L$ and $b_L$ controlled its position and slope:
\begin{equation*}
p_L(f)=\left\{1+\exp\left[-a_L-b_L(f-0.18)/0.01\right]\right\}^{-1},
\qquad b_L>0.
\end{equation*}
We fitted this curve by binomial likelihood and used the same seed blocks
to bootstrap its midpoint and width. Fitted standard errors were bootstrap
standard deviations. Both analyses measured uncertainty for the tested fractions and
observation period. Neither included error from interpolation, the
choice of additional fractions, or observing the market for longer.

We selected examples of traders' decisions across the grid using the
measured time to a majority or final Sell fraction to choose each run.
Selection preceded inspection of the images.
The activation-fraction panels included a
run without a majority at $f=0.15$, both outcomes at $f=0.175$, and runs with a majority at $f=0.185$ and 0.20 under $h=\infty$.
Among runs that reached a majority, we selected the run nearest the
group's median onset time. Among runs without a majority, we selected
the run nearest the group's median final Sell fraction. The dealer panels
required a single seed that transitioned at $h=0.1$, 1, and $\infty$ for
$f=0.20$. Among eligible seeds, selection minimized the summed distance
from the three capacity-specific median onsets. Both panel sets showed
snapshots at activation and at offsets 500, 1,500, and 3,000, allowing the
spatial trajectories to be compared at common elapsed times.
The examples therefore had typical onset times for their groups with
a majority and typical final Sell fractions for their groups without
one. Individual runs illustrated how groups of sellers developed, while
probabilities across runs measured how often a majority occurred.
