\section{Methods}

\subsection{Three-state cellular automaton}

We represented the synthetic market as $L$ rows and $L$ columns, with
$(L-2)^2$ traders in the interior, and recorded each trader's intended
action as Buy ($s=0$), Hold ($s=1$), or Sell ($s=2$). Technical traders
occupied selected positions along the edge and formed their intentions
from the dealer's quoted price and recent price history, while the
remaining edge positions stayed in Hold. Each interior trader formed its
local decision from the intentions of its four neighbors above, below,
to the left, and to the right. The decision rules were totalistic: each
trader's next intention depended only on the sum of these four
neighboring states~\cite{packard1985two}, so for interior traders next
to the edge this sum could include the intentions of technical traders.
At each step $t$, the technical traders updated their intended actions
using the latest quoted price and price history, and all interior
traders then updated theirs together using the previous interior states
and the newly updated edge states. We denoted the four neighbors of
trader $i$ by $\partial i$ and the state supplied by neighbor $j$ at
step $t$ by $s_j^{\rm in}(t)$, and the totalistic rule used their sum
$c_i(t)$, defined by:
\begin{equation}
  c_i(t)=\sum_{j\in\partial i}s_j^{\rm in}(t)\in\{0,1,\ldots,8\}.
  \label{eq:neighborhood-sum}
\end{equation}

Each totalistic decision rule assigned an intended action to each of the
nine possible neighbor sums. For a sum $k\in\{0,1,\ldots,8\}$, we
denoted the trader's next intended action by $a_k\in\{0,1,2\}$, using
the codes for Buy, Hold, and Sell. Specifying these nine outputs defined
the complete rule, giving $3^9=19{,}683$ possible rules for the
four-neighbor model. We identified each rule by its Wolfram rule number
$R$, with the outputs written as base-three digits from $a_8$ on the
left to $a_0$ on the
right~\cite{wolfram1984universality,packard1985two}, so the rule number
was defined by:
\begin{equation}
  R=\sum_{k=0}^{8}a_k3^k.
  \label{eq:rule-index}
\end{equation}
We used Rule~19280, $(222110002)_3$, as the baseline and Rule~19286,
$(222110022)_3$, for the selected traders after the rule switch. The two
rules differed only in their response to neighbor sum $k=1$,
corresponding to three neighbors intending to Buy and one intending to
Hold. For this configuration Rule~19280 assigned Buy and Rule~19286
assigned Sell as the trader's next intended action, whereas all other
neighbor sums produced the same intended action under both rules. Before
each run, we specified an approximate fraction $f$ of interior traders
to receive Rule~19286 later in the simulation and selected
$\operatorname{round}(f(L-2)^2)$ traders uniformly without replacement.
We initialized the interior with $\operatorname{round}(0.05(L-2)^2)$
traders intending to Buy and the same number intending to Sell, at
positions chosen uniformly without replacement, and all remaining
interior traders began in Hold. All interior traders initially used
Rule~19280, allowing the market to develop before the scheduled rule
switch. At the switch, the selected traders received Rule~19286 and
first used it to determine their next intended action, while all other
interior traders continued using Rule~19280. Assigning the new rule did
not execute a trade or reset any trader's cash or shares.

\subsection{Orders, trader resources, and completed trades}

A trader's intended action generated a desired order, but completing the
order required cash or shares and a counterparty. Each interior trader
and technical trader held cash $w_i$ and an integer number of shares
$q_i$. A Buy action requested one share, a Sell action offered one
share, and a Hold action generated no order. All completed trades within
a step used the same base execution price, and a buy required enough
cash to pay the execution price and any transaction fee, whereas a sale
required at least one share. We allowed sales below a trader's average
purchase price $\bar c_i$, so the model did not impose the reluctance to
realize losses described in behavioral
studies~\cite{shefrin1985disposition,odean1998investors}. We classified
desired orders that could not be completed because the trader lacked
cash or shares as \emph{trader suppression}. We allowed traders' Buy and
Sell intentions to influence their neighbors even when the traders
lacked the resources to carry them out, so cash and share holdings
limited completed trades but did not determine which intentions entered
neighboring decisions.

The dealer acted as market maker by using its own cash and shares to
complete trades that could not be matched between traders, and its
finite reserves could leave some orders unfilled. We denoted the total
number of interior and technical traders by $N_{\rm tr}$ and the dealer
depth, the initial dealer shares per trader, by $h$. At the initial
price $P_0=100$, the dealer started with $H=\operatorname{round}(hN_{\rm
tr})$ shares and cash $P_0H$. For positive reserves, we required trading
to preserve the product of the dealer's trading cash and shares,
following a constant-product market-making
rule~\cite{angeris2022constant}. The dealer could not borrow cash or
shares and retained at least one share. At the start of step $t$, its
cash and share reserves were $W_{t-1}$ and $Q_{t-1}$, with product $K$.
We denoted a proposed net purchase from the dealer by $x$, positive when
traders bought shares from the dealer and negative when they sold shares
to it, so at base execution price $F_t(x)$ the dealer's trading reserves
would contain $Q_{t-1}-x$ shares and cash $W_{t-1}+F_t(x)x$. Preserving
the reserve product required:
\begin{gather*}
  K=W_{t-1}Q_{t-1},\\
  [W_{t-1}+F_t(x)x][Q_{t-1}-x]=K.
\end{gather*}
For a nonzero net trade, solving this balance for the execution price
gave:
\begin{equation}
  F_t(x)=\frac{W_{t-1}}{Q_{t-1}-x},\qquad Q_{t-1}-x\geq1.
  \label{eq:execution-price}
\end{equation}
When $x=0$, we used $F_t(0)=W_{t-1}/Q_{t-1}$, so opposing trader orders
could still trade without changing the dealer's reserves. We denoted the
transaction fee per completed order, as a fraction of its base execution
price, by $\tau$, and used $\tau=0$ except in the fee comparisons
described below. For each proposed net trade $x$, we counted Buy orders
backed by enough cash to pay $(1+\tau)F_t(x)$ as $B_t(x)$ and Sell
orders backed by at least one share as $S_t(x)$. These were the eligible
orders at the proposed price. We first compared eligible buys and sells
at $F_t(0)$, and their difference determined the direction $d_t$, with
$+1$ denoting net purchases from the dealer, $-1$ net sales to the
dealer, and zero no net trade:
\begin{equation*}
  d_t=\operatorname{sign}\{B_t(0)-S_t(0)\}.
\end{equation*}
For $d_t\ne0$, we considered nonnegative integer trade sizes $k$ in the
selected direction, subject to two requirements. The dealer had to
retain at least one share after trading, and at the proposed execution
price, eligible orders in the selected direction had to exceed orders on
the opposite side by at least $k$. We chose the largest size satisfying
both requirements, giving the net purchase $x_t$:
\begin{equation}
 x_t=d_t\max\left\{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq0}:
 Q_{t-1}-d_tk\geq1,\quad
 d_t[B_t(d_tk)-S_t(d_tk)]\geq k\right\}.
 \label{eq:dealer-buy-excess}
\end{equation}
We set $x_t=0$ when $d_t=0$. At the selected price $F_t(x_t)$, we
denoted the eligible counts by $B_t$ and $S_t$, and the numbers of
completed trader buys $n_t^B$ and sells $n_t^S$ were then given by:
\begin{equation}
 n_t^B=\min\{B_t,S_t+x_t\},\qquad n_t^S=n_t^B-x_t.
 \label{eq:dealer-sell-excess}
\end{equation}
All eligible orders on the smaller side completed, and when orders on
the larger side could not all complete, reproducible priorities
determined which were filled. We called eligible orders left unfilled
\emph{dealer rejection}. Because a larger net trade could violate the
dealer's minimum share reserve or change the execution price so that the
eligible orders no longer supported the proposed quantity, dealer
rejection did not imply that the dealer had exhausted its cash. The
normalized net purchase $\phi_t$ described the completed orders at each
step but did not separately set price, and it was defined by:
\begin{equation}
 \phi_t=\frac{x_t}{N_{\rm tr}}.
 \label{eq:imbalance}
\end{equation}

All completed orders used the common base execution price
$F_t=F_t(x_t)$, so the dealer's remaining shares $Q_t$ and trading cash
$W_t$ were:
\begin{equation}
 Q_t=Q_{t-1}-x_t,\qquad W_t=W_{t-1}+F_tx_t.
 \label{eq:dealer-ledger}
\end{equation}
After settlement, we set the dealer's quoted price $P_t$ equal to its
remaining trading cash divided by its remaining shares, and substituting
the base execution price into these balances gave:
\begin{equation}
 P_t=\frac{W_t}{Q_t}
     =P_{t-1}\left(\frac{Q_{t-1}}{Q_{t-1}-x_t}\right)^2.
 \label{eq:price-update}
\end{equation}
This quoted price supplied the price signal used by technical traders
and differed from the base execution price whenever $x_t\ne0$. For each
completed order, a buyer paid $(1+\tau)F_t$ and a seller received
$(1-\tau)F_t$, and the dealer collected the fees in a separate cash
account $G_t$, initialized at zero and excluded from the trading
reserves used to set prices:
\begin{equation}
 G_t=G_{t-1}+\tau F_t(n_t^B+n_t^S),\qquad G_0=0.
 \label{eq:dealer-fees}
\end{equation}
No fee was charged on an unfilled order. Cash was conserved across
traders, dealer trading reserves, and the fee account, except at
scheduled changes in trader resources, and shares were conserved across
traders and the dealer, except at scheduled share interventions. In
exact arithmetic, the constant reserve product $W_tQ_t=K$ meant that an
isolated sequence of trades returning the dealer to its initial share
reserve also restored its initial trading cash. Such a round trip could
not extract net cash from the dealer and, at positive $\tau$, paid fees
to the dealer, although this result did not rule out gains from trading
with other traders. Dealer reserves determined both the funding
available for trades and the response of the quoted price to net
trading. The comparison with large reserves used dealer depth $h=100$,
whereas at dealer depth $h=0$ the dealer held no trading cash or shares.
Only opposing trader orders backed by sufficient resources could then be
matched, the price remained at $P_0$, and fees collected in this case
did not fund dealer trading. This separate case therefore provided a
fixed-price control (Algorithm~\ref{alg:dealer-clearing}).

\subsection{Technical traders and price feedback}

Technical traders brought changes in the dealer's quoted price into
neighboring decisions. We placed these traders at one quarter of the
positions along the edge of the grid, while the remaining edge positions
stayed in Hold. The technical traders followed a contrarian strategy of
the Bollinger-band type, in which each technical trader compared the
quoted price with a band around a recent price average. A price below
the lower limit generated a Buy intention, a price above the upper limit
generated a Sell intention, and prices within the band, including its
limits, generated Hold. These intentions influenced neighboring interior
traders and supplied orders for the next trading step. This contrarian
response contrasted with the trend-following moving-average rules often
tested in market data~\cite{brock1992simple}. The reference price
$\mu_t$ was an exponentially weighted moving average of the dealer's
quoted price, and the band width used $\sigma_t$, an exponentially
smoothed absolute departure from this average rather than a standard
deviation. Both quantities used the smoothing parameter $\alpha=1/60$.
After settlement, we first updated the average using the new quoted
price $P_t$ and then updated the departure:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \mu_t &= \mu_{t-1}+\alpha(P_t-\mu_{t-1}),\\
 \sigma_t &= \sigma_{t-1}+\alpha\bigl(|P_t-\mu_t|-\sigma_{t-1}\bigr).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:adaptive-band}
\end{equation}
We initialized the average at $\mu_0=P_0$ and the departure at
$\sigma_0=0$. Each technical trader received a fixed multiplier $k_j\sim
U[0.5,1.5]$, giving lower and upper limits $\mu_t-k_j\sigma_t$ and
$\mu_t+k_j\sigma_t$.

At the start of each step, we applied any scheduled changes to decision
rules or trader resources, and traders then submitted orders from their
existing intentions. After settlement, technical traders updated their
bands and intentions using the dealer's new quoted price, and interior
traders then updated simultaneously, using the previous intentions of
neighboring interior traders and the new intentions of adjacent
technical traders. The resulting intentions supplied orders for the
following step (Algorithm~\ref{alg:model-step}). To remove price
feedback, we kept every technical trader in Hold from initialization, so
these traders submitted no orders and communicated Hold to their
neighbors. Interior traders could still trade, and cash, shareholdings,
and the dealer's quoted price could still change, but those changes
could no longer influence interior intentions through the technical
traders. We compared this condition, \emph{technical traders held}, with
\emph{technical traders active}, in which they followed the band rule.
Both conditions began with the same starting market and used the same
interior traders selected for the rule switch, so the comparison tested
whether a Sell majority could develop from responses to neighbors
without feedback from prices. Figure legends abbreviate these conditions
as held and active.
\subsection{Paired rule-switch experiments}

We isolated the effect of the rule switch by comparing each switched
market with a no-switch control that retained the original rule. Each
pair began from the same starting market and used Rule~19280 for every
interior trader for 1,000 steps. At the start of step 1,001, we assigned
Rule~19286 to a preselected fraction $f$ of interior traders, who
retained their current intentions and financial resources, while the
control continued with Rule~19280. The two simulations therefore shared
the same history up to the rule switch. Each condition in the four main
comparisons used 50 starting markets (Supplementary
Table~\ref{tab:campaigns}). Every trader initially held 1,000 cash units
and ten shares, and each simulation continued for 3,000 steps after the
baseline. Additional comparisons tested other decision rules and a
one-time instruction to sell, and we also kept the technical traders'
band widths fixed after the rule switch, tested additional dealer
depths, and extended the observation period. The supplementary protocols
give the conditions and parameter values for these comparisons
(Appendix~\ref{app:revision-controls}).

We varied the fraction of traders given the new rule, market size, and
financial resources to compare the spread of Sell intentions with
changes in completed trades and prices. The rule-switch comparison used
a $64\times64$ grid, active technical traders, and dealer depth $h=100$
at fractions $f\in\{0.10,0.15,0.20,0.25\}$, whereas the dealer-depth
comparison used 11 fractions between 0.10 and 0.25 at eight dealer
depths $h$ from 0 to 100, with technical traders active or held. For the
size comparison, we used grid sizes $L\in\{32,48,64,96,128\}$, with
technical traders held at dealer depth $h=100$ or active at $h=1$ and
$h=100$. At a fixed dealer depth $h$, initial dealer cash and share
reserves increased in proportion to the number of traders. To test how
trader resources affected the response to the rule switch, we
independently multiplied each trader's current cash and shareholdings by
factors $g_w,g_q\in\{0.1,1,10\}$ at the switch, which gave nine
combinations, including unchanged holdings when both factors were 1.
Share counts were rounded to whole numbers, and the intervention did not
rescale the dealer's reserves. With other settings fixed, all nine
simulations shared the same history before the intervention and assigned
the new rule to the same traders. When technical traders were held,
changing resources could alter completed trades and prices but could not
alter interior intentions, because neither cash nor shareholdings
entered the decision rule. The supplementary material gives the full
parameter combinations and verification checks
(Appendix~\ref{app:supplement}).

\subsection{Market outcomes, statistical analysis, and selection of examples}

We measured the spread of Sell intentions using $m(t)$, the fraction of
interior traders in Sell at step $t$, and recorded a Sell majority if
$m(t)\geq0.5$ at least once during the observation period after the rule
switch. Onset time was the first step after the rule switch at which
this threshold was reached. For each condition, we estimated majority
probability as the fraction of runs that reached the threshold and
summarized onset times only for those runs. In the comparisons of dealer
depth, market size, and trader resources, we also averaged $m(t)$ over
the final 500 steps to describe intentions near the end of each run. For
the market-size comparison, we denoted each run's mean Sell fraction
over the final 500 steps by $\bar m$ and measured variation between
starting markets using the susceptibility $\chi_L$, defined as the
variance of $\bar m$ multiplied by the number of interior traders,
$(L-2)^2$~\cite{binder1981finite,landau2021guide}. With angle brackets
denoting averages over the 50 starting markets at a fixed grid size and
parameter combination, we calculated:
\begin{equation}
 \chi_L=(L-2)^2\left[\langle\bar m^2\rangle-
                         \langle\bar m\rangle^2\right].
 \label{eq:susceptibility}
\end{equation}
At a fixed grid size, a larger $\chi_L$ indicated greater variation in
the final mean Sell fraction between starting markets
(Eq.~\ref{eq:susceptibility}). For each market size $L$ and condition,
we estimated the fraction of traders assigned the new rule at which a
Sell majority formed in 25\%, 50\%, or 75\% of simulations within the
observation period, and we denoted these fractions by $f_{25}(L)$,
$f_{50}(L)$, and $f_{75}(L)$. We estimated these values with straight
lines between the measured probabilities at adjacent tested fractions
(linear interpolation), using the first upward crossing of each
probability level and retaining any decreases in the sampled
probabilities. The midpoint $f_{50}(L)$ corresponded to a majority
forming in half of the starting markets within the observation period.
The width $f_{75}(L)-f_{25}(L)$ measured the increase in the fraction
$f$ between majority probabilities of 0.25 and 0.75, so a smaller width
indicated a sharper response. We used 95\% Wilson confidence intervals
to describe uncertainty in estimated Sell-majority
probabilities~\cite{wilson1927probable}. Where shown, interquartile
bands, which described variation between runs, extended from the 25th to
the 75th percentile of outcomes across starting markets under the same
conditions.

A high Sell fraction did not necessarily produce a large price decline,
because the price response depended on completed trades and the dealer's
trading reserves. We measured price declines using maximum drawdown, the
largest proportional fall from the highest quoted price reached so far
within the observation period, including the quoted price immediately
before the rule switch. We used price crash to describe a large
simulated decline, without defining a threshold for classifying runs as
crashes. A Buy order was eligible if the trader had enough cash to pay
the selected execution price and any fee, and a Sell order was eligible
if the trader held at least one share. We counted ineligible orders as
trader suppression and eligible orders left unfilled as dealer
rejection. Where reported, we calculated the dealer-rejection fraction
as rejected orders divided by eligible orders, using each run's totals
over the observation period after the rule switch, and we assigned zero
when no orders were eligible. Suppressed and completed order counts
covered the same period. We summarized maximum drawdown and
dealer-rejection fractions using medians across starting markets under
the same conditions.

For the market-size comparison, we tested additional fractions of
traders assigned the new rule to locate the rise in Sell-majority
probability more precisely, aiming for at least five tested fractions
with estimated majority probabilities strictly between 5\% and 95\% for
each size and condition. Until this criterion was met, we added a
fraction halfway between adjacent tested values when the lower fraction
had a probability below 95\% and the higher fraction had a probability
above 5\%, allowing up to ten rounds and using the same 50 starting
markets at each added fraction. The number of fractions meeting the
criterion appears in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:finite-size-scaling}.
For each market size and condition, we drew 2,000 bootstrap samples of
50 starting markets with replacement, using the same selection of
starting markets at every tested fraction within a sample. In each
sample, we recalculated $f_{25}$, $f_{50}$, $f_{75}$, the width
$f_{75}-f_{25}$, and the maximum susceptibility over the tested
fractions, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of these estimates
defined the 95\% confidence intervals. We also fitted a smooth,
increasing logistic probability curve for each market size and condition
and compared its midpoint and width with the estimates from linear
interpolation. We modeled the Sell-majority probability as:
\begin{equation*}
p_L(f)=\left\{1+\exp\left[-a_L-b_L(f-0.18)/0.01\right]\right\}^{-1},
\qquad b_L>0.
\end{equation*}
The fitted parameters $a_L$ and $b_L$ controlled the curve's position
and steepness, while the constants 0.18 and 0.01 centered and scaled the
fraction $f$ for fitting. We estimated the parameters by maximizing the
binomial likelihood of the observed majority counts and refitted the
curve in each of the same 2,000 bootstrap samples, taking the standard
errors of its midpoint and width from the standard deviations of their
bootstrap estimates (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:revision-fits}). These
confidence intervals and standard errors described the uncertainty from
sampling starting markets, with the tested fractions and the observation
period held fixed, so they did not include uncertainty from selecting
the additional fractions. For the interpolated midpoints and widths,
they also left out the error of approximating the probability curve by
straight lines between tested fractions. These estimates did not
describe how the curve would change over a longer observation period,
which is a different measured outcome rather than an estimation error.

We selected examples of traders' decisions across the grid using the
measured time to a majority or final Sell fraction to choose each run,
and selection preceded inspection of the images, with the lower-numbered
starting market breaking equal distances from a group median. The newly
computed 3,000-step results supplied a run without a majority at
fraction $f=0.15$, both outcomes at $f=0.175$, and runs with a majority
at $f=0.185$ and 0.20, all at dealer depth $h=100$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:dose-panels}). Among runs that reached a majority, we
selected the run nearest the group's median onset time, whereas among
runs without a majority we selected the run nearest the group's median
final Sell fraction. The dealer panels required a single starting market
that reached a majority at dealer depths $h=0.1$, 1, and 100 for
fraction $f=0.20$, chosen among eligible starting markets to minimize
the summed distance from the three depth-specific median onsets. We
extended the five examples across fractions assigned the new rule to
10,000 steps after the rule switch, with snapshots at 0, 1,500, 3,000,
5,000, and 10,000 steps (Fig.~\ref{fig:dose-panels}). The dealer
comparisons retained their original 3,000-step duration and snapshots at
0, 500, 1,500, and 3,000 steps. Selection therefore represented typical
outcomes within each group over the original observation period, and a
market without a majority at that endpoint could reach one later.
Individual runs illustrated how groups of sellers developed, while
probabilities across runs measured how often a majority occurred.
