\section{Methods}
% ============================================================================

\subsection{Three-state cellular automaton}

Agents occupied an $L\times L$ square lattice and carried one of three states:
Buy ($s=0$), Hold ($s=1$), or Sell ($s=2$). We modeled the synchronous local
update as a cellular automaton in which only the $(L-2)^2$ strictly interior
cells followed the rule. For each interior cell $i$, with $\partial i$ its four
von Neumann neighbors, the neighborhood sum was:
\begin{equation}
  c_i(t)=\sum_{j\in\partial i}s_j(t)\in\{0,1,\ldots,8\}.
  \label{eq:neighborhood-sum}
\end{equation}
A totalistic rule table ${\cal R}$ maps each possible sum to one of the three
states. The nine outputs, written as ternary digits $a_j={\cal R}(j)$, define
the Wolfram rule index~\cite{wolfram1984universality,packard1985two}:
\begin{equation}
  R=\sum_{j=0}^{8}a_j3^j.
  \label{eq:rule-index}
\end{equation}
The numerical state labels therefore entered the dynamics through both the
neighborhood sum and the rule table. This restriction represents a trader
responding to a scalar score of local selling pressure, with Hold intermediate
between Buy and Sell; it is a deliberately minimal response rule, not a
utility-maximizing decision. Equal sums can hide distinct compositions: two
Holds and two Buys are indistinguishable from one Sell and three Buys. The
experiments therefore test this totalistic model class rather than all rules
on neighborhood compositions.

We used Rule~19280, $(222110002)_3$, as the baseline and Rule~19286,
$(222110022)_3$, as the activated rule. The tables differed only at $c=1$, so
a neighborhood with three Buy cells and one Hold cell returned Buy under
Rule~19280 and Sell under Rule~19286. For each run, we selected a fraction $f$
of interior sites uniformly without replacement and kept this target set
fixed, so that its sites changed to Rule~19286 only at the intervention step.
The initial interior contained $\operatorname{round}(0.05(L-2)^2)$ Buy sites
and as many Sell sites placed without replacement, with Hold elsewhere.
Randomness entered through the
initial states, target sites, active boundary sample, boundary band widths,
and finite-dealer rationing. Once these draws were fixed by the seed, the
subsequent dynamics were deterministic.

\subsection{Orders, trader books, and dealer clearing}

Every interior cell and every active boundary cell represented a trader. A
Buy state submitted one unit buy order, a Sell state submitted one unit sell
order, and a Hold state submitted no order. Trader $i$ carried cash $w_i$,
integer inventory $q_i$, and average acquisition cost $\bar c_i$. A buy was
eligible when $w_i\geq P_{t-1}$, and a sell was eligible when $q_i\geq1$. We
disabled the optional cost-basis restriction, which would have permitted a sale
only above the trader's average acquisition cost in the manner of the
disposition effect~\cite{shefrin1985disposition,odean1998investors}, so average
cost did not prevent a sale in the main experiments. An order that failed an
eligibility test was \emph{agent-suppressed}, which prevented execution
without changing the trader's CA state.

We let $B_t$ and $S_t$ denote the eligible buy and sell counts at the standing
quote $P_{t-1}$. Opposing flow of size $\min(B_t,S_t)$ cleared without using
dealer resources, and an explicit dealer handled only the unmatched residual.
We expressed dealer capacity as $h\geq0$ net units per trader. For $N_{\rm tr}$
traders, total capacity was $H=\operatorname{round}(hN_{\rm tr})$, so larger
$h$ supplied more dealer cash and inventory. A finite dealer began with cash
$W_0=P_0H$ at the initial quote $P_0$ and inventory $Q_0=H$, could not borrow,
and satisfied $0\leq Q_t\leq2H$. When buys exceeded sells, the executed buy
and sell counts $n_t^B$ and $n_t^S$ were:
\begin{align}
 n_t^S &= S_t, &
 n_t^B &= S_t+\min(B_t-S_t,Q_{t-1}).
 \label{eq:dealer-buy-excess}
\end{align}
When sells exceeded buys, the corresponding counts were:
\begin{align}
 n_t^B &= B_t, &
 n_t^S &= B_t+\min\!\left(S_t-B_t,2H-Q_{t-1},
                 \left\lfloor\frac{W_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}}\right\rfloor\right).
 \label{eq:dealer-sell-excess}
\end{align}
Eligible units beyond these bounds were \emph{dealer-rejected}. When rationing
was required, a counter-based score keyed by seed, step, trader identity, and
side gave each eligible trader a reproducible priority, and including the step
in the key prevented one trader from retaining priority over time. Filled
orders updated trader books at $P_{t-1}$, and the opposite transfers updated
the dealer ledger:
\begin{align}
  Q_t &= Q_{t-1}+n_t^S-n_t^B, &
  W_t &= W_{t-1}+P_{t-1}(n_t^B-n_t^S).
  \label{eq:dealer-ledger}
\end{align}
Total cash and shares over traders plus dealer were conserved when no
balance-sheet intervention occurred. The $h=\infty$ limit started from a zero
dealer ledger, permitted borrowing and shorting, and filled every eligible
order, whereas $h=0$ allowed only opposing trader orders to clear. Both were
limits of the same clearing rule (Algorithm~\ref{alg:dealer-clearing}).

\subsection{Price feedback and the held-boundary control}

Only executed orders moved the price. We defined normalized executed imbalance
as:
\begin{equation}
  \phi_t=\frac{n_t^B-n_t^S}{N_{\rm tr}}.
  \label{eq:imbalance}
\end{equation}
Positive imbalance represented net buying and raised the quote, and negative
imbalance represented net selling and lowered it. The quote update, with
price-impact coefficient $\lambda$, was:
\begin{equation}
  P_t=P_{t-1}\exp(\lambda\phi_t).
  \label{eq:price-update}
\end{equation}
The main experiments used $P_0=100$ and $\lambda=0.02$. Active boundary traders
compared the new quote with an exponential moving center $\mu_t$ and an
exponential moving absolute deviation $\sigma_t$, which were updated with
smoothing parameter $\alpha=1/60$ as:
\begin{align}
 \mu_t &= \mu_{t-1}+\alpha(P_t-\mu_{t-1}), &
 \sigma_t &= \sigma_{t-1}+\alpha(|P_t-\mu_t|-\sigma_{t-1}),
 \label{eq:adaptive-band}
\end{align}
Boundary trader $j$ had a fixed half-width
$k_j\sim U[k(1-\delta),k(1+\delta)]$, with $k=1$ and $\delta=0.5$, and entered
Buy below $\mu_t-k_j\sigma_t$, Sell above $\mu_t+k_j\sigma_t$, and Hold inside
the band. One quarter of perimeter cells were active, and the rest stayed at
Hold.

The simulation wrote the new boundary state before updating the interior. The
complete feedback sequence was:
\begin{equation*}
 \text{CA state}\rightarrow\text{orders}\rightarrow\text{dealer fills}
 \rightarrow P_t\rightarrow\text{boundary state}\rightarrow\text{CA state}.
\end{equation*}
The implementation applied rule activation, any balance-sheet intervention,
eligibility tests, clearing, settlement, price formation, boundary
classification, and the synchronous CA update in that order
(Algorithms~\ref{alg:model-step} and~\ref{alg:dealer-clearing}). The matched
\emph{held-boundary} control retained the same initial lattice, target sites,
active boundary coordinates, band draws, and initial books and dealer, and it
used the same order, clearing, and price rules. Before each interior update,
however, every active boundary state
was set to Hold. Financial variables continued to evolve but could not affect
the lattice, so this control isolated the lattice dynamics from price feedback.

\subsection{Paired rule-switch experiments}

Every paired experiment began with Rule~19280 at every interior site and
evolved for $T_{\rm pre}=1{,}000$ steps before any rule changed. At the top of
step $T_{\rm pre}+1$, the switched path activated Rule~19286 at the
preselected fraction $f$, whereas the matched no-switch path left those target
sites on Rule~19280, and neither path reset the lattice, quote, adaptive
bands, trader books, or dealer. Because the two paths were identical through
the full lattice state $X_{T_{\rm pre}}$ and the next quote came from that
common state, the first possible difference occurred during the following
interior update, and we verified these pre-switch equalities bit for bit for
every pair. Four production campaigns used this protocol
(Table~\ref{tab:campaigns}), each with 3,000 post-activation steps, 50 seeds,
and traders that began with cash 1,000 and 10 shares. These values were fixed
constants within each production campaign. The repository includes pilot and
full modes, but does not establish a prospective power calculation or an
independent preregistration of the horizon; we make neither claim. Fifty
seeds give a binomial standard error of about 0.07 near probability 0.5.
The selected
paired-switch campaign used $L=64$, an unlimited dealer, an endogenous
boundary, and $f\in\{0.10,0.15,0.20,0.25\}$. The dealer-capacity screen kept
$L=64$, sampled the fraction grid 0.10, 0.15, 0.175, 0.185, 0.190, 0.195,
0.200, 0.205, 0.210, 0.225, and 0.250, and took dealer capacities 0, 0.1, 0.5,
1, 2, 5, 10, and $\infty$ under both boundaries. The finite-size experiment
used $L\in\{32,48,64,96,128\}$ with 12 fractions from 0.140 to 0.250 under a
held boundary with $h=\infty$ and endogenous boundaries with $h=\infty$ and
$h=1$, so absolute dealer capacity, defined per trader, scaled with lattice
size. The balance-sheet experiment multiplied current cash and current shares
independently by $g_w,g_q\in\{0.1,1,10\}$ at the same top-of-step event as
rule activation, rounded
shares to integers, and crossed the nine multiplier pairs with
$f\in\{0.170,0.175,0.180,0.185\}$, $h\in\{0.1,1,\infty\}$, and both
boundaries. All nine interventions within a $(f,h,\text{boundary},\text{seed})$
cell shared an identical pre-intervention path, and under the held boundary
their full lattice paths also had to remain identical afterward, which
supplied an exact null test for a direct effect of financial variables on the
interior rule.

Simulations ran in Julia 1.12 with the package environment pinned by the
repository manifest. Original campaigns used 12 simulation threads and one
garbage-collection thread; revision campaigns used four or eight simulation
threads and one garbage-collection thread. Bootstrap analysis and generated
LaTeX tables used Python, with NumPy, SciPy, and pandas versions recorded in
\texttt{code/revision/requirements.txt}. A seeded Xoshiro generator~\cite{blackman2021scrambled} produced the
setup draws, the finite-size and balance-sheet campaigns wrote an atomic
checkpoint per cell and resumed from it, and aggregation reconstructed a fixed
row order, so thread scheduling and restart order did not alter the results.
Unit tests covered parameter validation, finite-dealer limits, time-varying
priorities, conservation, delayed rule activation, the held-boundary control,
and the unlimited-dealer limit in which every eligible order executes, and
step-by-step tests compared the optimized CA kernel with the general
totalistic solver under homogeneous and heterogeneous rule fields. The code,
environment manifest, derived data, and figure scripts are available at
\url{https://github.com/varnerlab/Wolfram-markets-abm-paper}.

\subsection{Outcomes, statistical analysis, and prespecified panels}

The order parameter $m(t)$ was the strictly interior Sell fraction, so higher
$m(t)$ meant that a larger share of the interior occupied the Sell state. We
called a run transitioned when $m(t)\geq0.5$ at least once after activation
and defined onset as the first such crossing. We reported transition
probability, onset conditional on crossing, the final 500-step mean of $m(t)$,
maximum post-activation drawdown, agent-suppression fractions, and the fraction
of eligible orders rejected by the dealer. At fixed $(L,f,h)$, the ensemble
susceptibility, a standard finite-size
quantity~\cite{binder1981finite,landau2021guide}, measured between-seed
fluctuations in the final-window mean $\bar m$, with angle brackets denoting
the average over seeds and $(L-2)^2$ the number of interior sites:
\begin{equation}
 \chi_L=(L-2)^2\left[\langle\bar m^2\rangle-
                         \langle\bar m\rangle^2\right].
 \label{eq:susceptibility}
\end{equation}
We linearly interpolated the sampled transition curve to estimate the
finite-horizon midpoint $f_{50}(L)$ and width $f_{75}(L)-f_{25}(L)$.
Revision experiments bisected intervals intersecting the 5--95\% response
until every size and boundary/capacity condition had at least five fractions
with empirical probability strictly between 0.05 and 0.95. The original 50
seeds were retained at every added fraction. We resampled whole seeds across
all fractions 2,000 times and recomputed the crossings, width, and sampled
susceptibility maximum. Percentile intervals therefore preserve dependence
between fractions, but condition on the selected grid and horizon. They do
not measure interpolation bias or uncertainty about an asymptotic limit.
As a shape sensitivity check, we fitted a monotone logistic curve
\begin{equation*}
p_L(f)=\left\{1+\exp\left[-a_L-b_L(f-0.18)/0.01\right]\right\}^{-1},
\qquad b_L>0,
\end{equation*}
by binomial likelihood and bootstrapped its midpoint and width using the same seed
blocks; fitted standard errors are bootstrap standard deviations. Empirical
curves were not forced to be monotone; interpolation used their first upward
crossing. Binomial proportions used 95\% Wilson
intervals~\cite{wilson1927probable}, and continuous summaries used
interquartile ranges unless noted otherwise. All finite-size quantities
remain descriptions of a finite observation window.

Additional controls applied a one-time Sell pulse at the same target sites,
screened all 18 single-entry neighbors of Rule~19280, froze the boundary
band deviation at activation, varied $\lambda$, and extended no-switch and
held-boundary trajectories. Their sample sizes, timing, and outcomes are
reported in Appendix~\ref{sec:revision-controls}. These are follow-up
sensitivity analyses motivated by review, distinct from the original four
campaigns. Displayed lattice runs were selected from ensemble summaries before
inspection of their images, so the spatial illustrations do not determine
which ensemble results are reported.

We chose every run displayed in the time-sliced market panels from these
ensemble summaries before inspecting any lattice image. The
activation-fraction panels included a nontransitioning $f=0.15$ run, both
outcome groups at $f=0.175$, and transitioning runs at $f=0.185$ and 0.20
under $h=\infty$. Within each transitioning group we chose the run whose onset was nearest
the subgroup median, whereas within each nontransitioning group we chose the
run whose final Sell fraction was nearest it. The dealer-capacity panels required one seed
that transitioned at $h=0.1$, 1, and $\infty$ for $f=0.20$, and among eligible
seeds we minimized the summed distance from the three capacity-specific
median onsets. Both sets of panels displayed the lattice at offsets 0, 500,
1,500, and 3,000 after activation, so every row used the same clock.
