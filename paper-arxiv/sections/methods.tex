\section{Methods}

\subsection{Three-state cellular automaton}

We represented local trader interactions on an $L\times L$ square
lattice, where interior traders responded to their immediate neighbors.
Agents occupied Buy
($s=0$), Hold ($s=1$), or Sell ($s=2$). Only the $(L-2)^2$ interior
cells followed the cellular-automaton rule. Each interior cell updated
synchronously from its four nearest neighbors, denoted by $\partial i$.
Their state sum $c_i(t)$ defined the local input. A totalistic table
${\cal R}$ assigned one output $a_j={\cal R}(j)$ to each possible sum.
The sum represents a scalar score of selling pressure, with Hold between
Buy and Sell, but discards neighborhood composition. Two Holds and two
Buys receive the same response as one Sell and three Buys.
Changing one table entry therefore altered the response to a specified
neighbor sum while preserving the other eight responses. Neighborhoods
with the same sum remained equivalent under the altered rule.
The local input and the Wolfram index $R$ encoding the nine
outputs were defined by the expressions~\cite{wolfram1984universality,packard1985two}:
\begin{gather}
  c_i(t)=\sum_{j\in\partial i}s_j(t)\in\{0,1,\ldots,8\},
  \label{eq:neighborhood-sum}\\
  R=\sum_{j=0}^{8}a_j3^j.
  \label{eq:rule-index}
\end{gather}

Rule~19280, $(222110002)_3$, supplied the baseline response.
The activated Rule~19286, $(222110022)_3$, differed only at $c=1$.
A neighborhood containing three Buys and one Hold therefore returned Buy
under the baseline and Sell under activation. Before each run, we selected
a fraction $f$ of interior sites uniformly without replacement as the target
set for the later switch. The initial interior contained
$\operatorname{round}(0.05(L-2)^2)$ Buy sites and the same number of Sell
sites, with Hold elsewhere. Initial states were placed without replacement.
A seed fixed the initial placements, target sites, active boundary
sample, and boundary band multipliers. It also fixed the priorities used
when eligible orders exceeded dealer capacity. Once these choices were
made, the market evolved deterministically. Delaying activation allowed
the market to develop under the baseline rule before the response changed.
At the intervention, each targeted trader retained its current state and
holdings but used the altered rule in subsequent updates.

\subsection{Orders, trader books, and dealer clearing}

A trader needed sufficient cash or shares to submit an eligible order,
and that order needed a counterparty to execute. Every interior cell and
active boundary cell represented a trader with cash
$w_i$, integer inventory $q_i$, and average acquisition cost $\bar c_i$.
Buy and Sell states requested one unit on their respective sides of the
market; Hold requested no trade. Eligibility depended on the resources
needed to settle at the standing quote $P_{t-1}$. A buy required
$w_i\geq P_{t-1}$, while a sell required $q_i\geq1$. The main experiments
disabled the optional cost-basis restriction, which permits sales only above
average acquisition cost in the manner of the disposition
effect~\cite{shefrin1985disposition,odean1998investors}.
An ineligible order was recorded as \emph{agent-suppressed}. Suppression
prevented execution without changing the trader's cellular-automaton state.
A trader could therefore remain in Sell after exhausting its shares,
or in Buy without enough cash to purchase a share. Its neighbors still
responded to that state during the lattice update. We passed eligible
orders to the dealer clearing procedure, which determined how many
could fill against opposing trader orders and available dealer resources
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:dealer-clearing}).

Eligible orders first cleared against opposing trader flow. With $B_t$
eligible buys and $S_t$ eligible sells, $\min(B_t,S_t)$ units on each side
executed without using dealer resources. The dealer handled the remaining
imbalance up to its cash and inventory limits. Capacity $h\geq0$ specified
net units per trader, giving total capacity
$H=\operatorname{round}(hN_{\rm tr})$ for $N_{\rm tr}$ traders. A finite
dealer began with cash $W_0=P_0H$ and inventory $Q_0=H$. It could not borrow
and had to satisfy $0\leq Q_t\leq2H$. When buys exceeded sells, executed
counts were:
\begin{align}
 n_t^S &= S_t, &
 n_t^B &= S_t+\min(B_t-S_t,Q_{t-1}).
 \label{eq:dealer-buy-excess}
\end{align}
When sells exceeded buys, execution also depended on the dealer's remaining
inventory space and ability to pay for shares:
\begin{align}
 n_t^B &= B_t, &
 n_t^S &= B_t+\min\!\left(S_t-B_t,2H-Q_{t-1},
                 \left\lfloor\frac{W_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}}\right\rfloor\right).
 \label{eq:dealer-sell-excess}
\end{align}
Eligible orders beyond these limits were \emph{dealer-rejected}.
A counter-based score keyed by seed, step, trader identity, and side set the
priority for rationing. Including the step allowed priority to change over
time while keeping it reproducible across paired runs. Settlement at
$P_{t-1}$ updated trader holdings and the opposing dealer transfers:
\begin{align}
  Q_t &= Q_{t-1}+n_t^S-n_t^B, &
  W_t &= W_{t-1}+P_{t-1}(n_t^B-n_t^S).
  \label{eq:dealer-ledger}
\end{align}
These transfers conserved total cash and shares across traders and dealer,
except at an intentional balance-sheet intervention. The unlimited limit
$h=\infty$ started from a zero dealer ledger and permitted borrowing and
shorting to fill every eligible order. At $h=0$, only opposing trader orders
could clear. Both limits used the same clearing procedure
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:dealer-clearing}).

\subsection{Price feedback and the held-boundary control}

Executed imbalance determined the price signal received by boundary
traders. We normalized the difference between filled buys and sells
by the fixed trader count $N_{\rm tr}$ to obtain imbalance $\phi_t$.
Positive imbalance raised the quote, negative imbalance lowered it,
and balanced execution left it unchanged. The exponential update used
price-impact coefficient $\lambda=0.02$ and initial quote $P_0=100$.
Active boundary traders compared each new quote with a moving center
$\mu_t$ and moving absolute deviation $\sigma_t$. Each trader had a
fixed multiplier $k_j\sim U[k(1-\delta),k(1+\delta)]$, with $k=1$
and $\delta=0.5$. Its decision band extended from
$\mu_t-k_j\sigma_t$ to $\mu_t+k_j\sigma_t$. The trader entered Buy
below the band, Sell above it, and Hold inside it. One quarter of
perimeter cells were active; the others stayed at Hold.
Boundary traders responded to departures from a moving price
reference. Their new states entered the update of adjacent interior cells,
providing the route by which executions could affect local behavior.
Imbalance, price, and the moving band statistics followed
these updates, with smoothing parameter $\alpha=1/60$:
\begin{gather}
  \phi_t=\frac{n_t^B-n_t^S}{N_{\rm tr}},
  \label{eq:imbalance}\\
  P_t=P_{t-1}\exp(\lambda\phi_t),
  \label{eq:price-update}\\
  \mu_t=\mu_{t-1}+\alpha(P_t-\mu_{t-1}),\qquad
  \sigma_t=\sigma_{t-1}+\alpha(|P_t-\mu_t|-\sigma_{t-1}).
  \label{eq:adaptive-band}
\end{gather}

Orders came from the current trader states, while the resulting price
influenced the next interior update
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:model-step}).
At each step, rule activation and any financial intervention preceded
eligibility tests, clearing, and settlement. Executed orders then generated
the new quote. Boundary traders responded to the quote before the interior cells updated
synchronously. The complete step followed the sequence:
\begin{equation*}
 \text{lattice state}\rightarrow\text{orders}\rightarrow\text{dealer fills}
 \rightarrow P_t\rightarrow\text{boundary state}\rightarrow\text{lattice state}.
\end{equation*}
The \emph{held-boundary} control removed the price-to-behavior connection by
setting every active boundary trader to Hold before each interior update.
It retained the same initial lattice, target sites, active coordinates,
band draws, trader holdings, and dealer. Orders still passed through the
same eligibility, clearing, and price rules.
Cash, inventory, and price could still change, but none could alter
the held-boundary lattice. The interior then evolved through neighbor
responses alone, allowing us to determine whether the rule change could
spread to a majority without a price signal (Algorithms~\ref{alg:model-step} and~\ref{alg:dealer-clearing}).

\subsection{Paired rule-switch experiments}

We introduced the rule change into paired market paths with a common
history. Both paths used Rule~19280 at every interior site for
$T_{\rm pre}=1{,}000$ baseline steps. At the start of step
$T_{\rm pre}+1$, one path activated Rule~19286 at the preselected fraction
$f$. The matched control left those sites on Rule~19280.
Neither path reset the lattice, quote, adaptive bands, trader holdings, or
dealer. Their common state $X_{T_{\rm pre}}$ also generated the first quote
after activation, so the earliest possible difference occurred in the
following interior update. We checked the shared pre-switch histories and
first post-switch quote bit for bit in every pair.
Each switched path thus began with the same trader resources and
neighbor configurations as its control. The four production
campaigns used 50 seeds and 3,000 post-activation steps, with initial trader
cash of 1,000 and inventory of ten shares. Additional controls extended this
protocol to other rules, a matched Sell pulse, frozen boundary bands,
different impact coefficients, and longer horizons.
The supplementary protocols specify which part of the intervention
or feedback was changed in each control (Appendix~\ref{app:revision-controls}).

We varied the activation fraction, lattice size, and financial resources
to determine when the rule change spread and how much selling executed (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:campaigns}).
The selected switch
experiment used $L=64$, an unlimited dealer, and an endogenous boundary at
four activation fractions. A finer screen crossed the fraction with eight
dealer capacities under both boundary conditions. The finite-size campaign
used $L\in\{32,48,64,96,128\}$ under a held boundary with $h=\infty$ and
endogenous boundaries with $h=1$ or $\infty$. Because capacity was defined
per trader, absolute dealer resources increased with lattice size.
The financial campaign multiplied current trader cash and shares independently
by $g_w,g_q\in\{0.1,1,10\}$ at activation, rounding shares to integers.
Its nine interventions shared the same pre-intervention path within each
$(f,h,\text{boundary},\text{seed})$ cell. Under the held boundary, they also
had to retain identical lattice trajectories afterward because neither cash nor shares entered the
interior update directly.
A discrepancy would therefore indicate an implementation error.
The appendix gives the full parameter grids and the checks used to verify
these paired and held-boundary relationships.

\subsection{Outcomes, statistical analysis, and prespecified panels}

We used the strictly interior Sell fraction $m(t)$ to measure collective
selling. Larger values indicated that more interior traders occupied Sell.
A run transitioned when $m(t)\geq0.5$ at least once after activation;
onset was the first crossing time, and transition probability was the
fraction of seeds that crossed. We summarized onset among crossing seeds
and used the final 500-step mean Sell fraction to describe each path
near the end of the observation window.
Between-seed variation in the final-window mean $\bar m$ defined the
ensemble susceptibility, a standard finite-size
quantity~\cite{binder1981finite,landau2021guide}:
\begin{equation}
 \chi_L=(L-2)^2\left[\langle\bar m^2\rangle-
                         \langle\bar m\rangle^2\right].
 \label{eq:susceptibility}
\end{equation}
Here, $(L-2)^2$ is the number of interior sites and angle brackets denote
the average over seeds. At a fixed size, higher susceptibility indicates
greater variation in the final-window mean across seeds. Linear
interpolation gave $f_q(L)$, the activation fraction at which the
transition probability reached $q/100$. The midpoint was $f_{50}(L)$,
and the width $f_{75}(L)-f_{25}(L)$ measured how much additional
activation separated probabilities of 0.25 and 0.75. A smaller width
therefore indicated a sharper response.
Interpolation used the first upward crossing without forcing the sampled
curve to be monotone. Binomial proportions used 95\% Wilson
intervals~\cite{wilson1927probable}; continuous summaries used interquartile
ranges unless otherwise specified.

A high Sell fraction need not produce a large price decline if the
requested orders cannot execute. We measured the decline by maximum
drawdown, the largest proportional fall from a running price peak
during the post-activation window. We recorded unfilled orders at the
stage where resources prevented execution. Agent suppression counted
desired orders excluded by insufficient trader cash or shares.
Dealer rejection counted eligible orders left unfilled after matching
because the dealer lacked cash or inventory capacity. In the production campaigns, the reported rejection fraction used
all eligible orders over the full run, including the baseline steps,
as its denominator, whereas the supplementary controls used
post-activation orders only. Higher values indicated that the dealer
prevented a larger share of available flow from executing. Financial summaries used medians
and interquartile ranges across seeds. Drawdown measured the price
consequence of filled imbalance, while suppression and rejection
identified the resource constraints that prevented desired orders from
contributing to that imbalance.

A coarse activation grid could miss the narrow range over which
majority probability changed. We refined each curve using a common
resolution criterion. Intervals intersecting the empirical 5--95\% response were
bisected until every size and boundary/capacity condition had at least five
fractions strictly inside that probability range. Each added fraction used
the original 50 seeds. We then resampled whole seeds across all fractions
2,000 times to estimate uncertainty in the crossings, width, and sampled
susceptibility maximum. These percentile intervals preserved dependence
between fractions and included reselection of the susceptibility peak.
A monotone logistic fit supplied a separate check on the assumed shape:
\begin{equation*}
p_L(f)=\left\{1+\exp\left[-a_L-b_L(f-0.18)/0.01\right]\right\}^{-1},
\qquad b_L>0.
\end{equation*}
We fitted this curve by binomial likelihood and used the same seed blocks
to bootstrap its midpoint and width. Fitted standard errors were bootstrap
standard deviations. Both analyses condition on the chosen grid and
horizon; neither estimates interpolation bias, adaptive-grid selection
uncertainty, or an infinite-time limit.

We selected lattice illustrations from ensemble summaries so that
crossing time or final Sell fraction determined which run was displayed.
Selection preceded inspection of the images.
The activation-fraction panels included a
nontransitioning $f=0.15$ run, both outcome groups at $f=0.175$, and
transitioning runs at $f=0.185$ and 0.20 under $h=\infty$.
Within a transitioning group, selection minimized the distance from the
subgroup median onset. Within a nontransitioning group, it minimized the
distance from the subgroup median final Sell fraction. The dealer panels
required a single seed that transitioned at $h=0.1$, 1, and $\infty$ for
$f=0.20$. Among eligible seeds, selection minimized the summed distance
from the three capacity-specific median onsets. Both panel sets showed
snapshots at activation and at offsets 500, 1,500, and 3,000, allowing the
spatial trajectories to be compared at common elapsed times.
The resulting panels followed typical onsets within the selected
crossing groups and typical final Sell fractions within the noncrossing
groups. Individual trajectories illustrated how the spatial patterns
developed; ensemble probabilities measured how often a majority occurred.
