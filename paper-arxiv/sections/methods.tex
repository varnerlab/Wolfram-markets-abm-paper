\section{Methods}

\subsection{Three-state cellular automaton}

We represented the synthetic market as $L$ rows and $L$ columns, with
$(L-2)^2$ traders in the interior, and recorded each trader's intended
action as Buy ($s=0$), Hold ($s=1$), or Sell ($s=2$). Technical traders
occupied selected positions along the edge and formed their intentions
from the dealer's quoted price and recent price history, while the
remaining edge positions stayed in Hold. Each interior trader formed its
local decision from the intentions of its four neighbors above, below,
to the left, and to the right. Each trader's next intention depended
only on the sum of these four neighboring states and not on its own
current state, so for interior traders next to the edge this sum could
include the intentions of technical traders. In the terms of Packard and
Wolfram, whose totalistic rules also add the cell's own state to the
sum, these are outer totalistic rules that ignore the cell's own
state~\cite{packard1985two}.
After trades settled at each step $t$, the technical traders updated
their intentions using the new quoted price and price history. All
interior traders then updated together using the previous interior
states and the newly updated edge states, producing intentions for
the next step's orders. We denoted the four neighbors of
trader $i$ by $\partial i$ and the state supplied by neighbor $j$ at
step $t$ by $s_j^{\rm in}(t)$, and the decision rule used their sum
$c_i(t)$, defined by:
\begin{equation}
  c_i(t)=\sum_{j\in\partial i}s_j^{\rm in}(t)\in\{0,1,\ldots,8\}.
  \label{eq:neighborhood-sum}
\end{equation}

Each decision rule assigned an intended action to each of the
nine possible neighbor sums. For a sum $k\in\{0,1,\ldots,8\}$, we
denoted the trader's next intended action by $a_k\in\{0,1,2\}$, using
the codes for Buy, Hold, and Sell. Specifying these nine outputs defined
the complete rule, giving $3^9=19{,}683$ possible rules for the
four-neighbor model. We identified each rule by its Wolfram rule number
$R$, with the outputs written as base-three digits from $a_8$ on the
left to $a_0$ on the
right~\cite{wolfram1984universality,packard1985two}, so the rule number
was defined by:
\begin{equation}
  R=\sum_{k=0}^{8}a_k3^k.
  \label{eq:rule-index}
\end{equation}
We used Rule~19280, $(222110002)_3$, as the baseline and Rule~19286,
$(222110022)_3$, for the selected traders after the rule switch. The two
rules differed only in their response to neighbor sum $k=1$,
corresponding to three neighbors intending to Buy and one intending to
Hold. For this configuration Rule~19280 assigned Buy and Rule~19286
assigned Sell as the trader's next intended action, whereas all other
neighbor sums produced the same intended action under both rules. Before
each run, we specified an approximate fraction $f$ of interior traders
to receive Rule~19286 later in the simulation and selected
$\operatorname{round}(f(L-2)^2)$ traders uniformly without replacement.
We initialized the interior with $\operatorname{round}(0.05(L-2)^2)$
traders intending to Buy and the same number intending to Sell, at
positions chosen uniformly without replacement, and all remaining
interior traders began in Hold. All interior traders initially used
Rule~19280, allowing the market to develop before the scheduled rule
switch. At the switch, the selected traders received Rule~19286 and
first used it to determine their next intended action, while all other
interior traders continued using Rule~19280. Assigning the new rule did
not execute a trade or reset any trader's cash or shares.

\subsection{Orders, trader resources, and completed trades}

A trader's intended action generated a desired order, but completing the
order required cash or shares and a counterparty. Each interior trader
and technical trader held cash $w_i$ and an integer number of shares
$q_i$. A Buy action requested one share, a Sell action offered one
share, and a Hold action generated no order. All completed trades within
a step used the same base execution price, and a buy required enough
cash to pay the execution price and any transaction fee, whereas a sale
required at least one share. We allowed sales below a trader's average
purchase price $\bar c_i$, so the model did not impose the reluctance to
realize losses described in behavioral
studies~\cite{shefrin1985disposition,odean1998investors}. We classified
desired orders that could not be completed because the trader lacked
cash or shares as \emph{trader suppression}. We allowed traders' Buy and
Sell intentions to influence their neighbors even when the traders
lacked the resources to carry them out, so cash and share holdings
limited completed trades but did not determine which intentions entered
neighboring decisions.

The dealer acted as market maker for orders unmatched between traders.
Clearing fixed the net trade direction, selected its largest feasible
size and price, then allocated orders at that price. For $N_{\rm tr}$
interior and technical traders, dealer depth $h$, the dealer's initial shares per trader, set initial shares
$H=\operatorname{round}(hN_{\rm tr})$ and cash $P_0H$, with initial
price $P_0=100$. The dealer could not borrow and, for positive reserves,
retained at least one share while preserving the product $K$ of trading
cash and shares~\cite{angeris2022constant}. At step $t$, the starting
reserves were cash $W_{t-1}$ and shares $Q_{t-1}$.
A proposed net purchase $x$ from the dealer was positive for trader
buys and negative for trader sales. Its base execution price $F_t(x)$
had to preserve the reserve product after the dealer exchanged $x$
shares for cash $F_t(x)x$, according to:
\begin{gather*}
  K=W_{t-1}Q_{t-1},\\
  [W_{t-1}+F_t(x)x][Q_{t-1}-x]=K.
\end{gather*}
For a nonzero proposed net trade, the corresponding price was:
\begin{equation}
  F_t(x)=\frac{W_{t-1}}{Q_{t-1}-x},\qquad Q_{t-1}-x\geq1.
  \label{eq:execution-price}
\end{equation}
For no net trade, we used $F_t(0)=W_{t-1}/Q_{t-1}$ to match opposing
orders without changing dealer reserves. The fee fraction of each
completed order's base price was $\tau$, with $\tau=0$ except in fee
comparisons. At each proposed price $F_t(x)$, the eligible counts were $B_t(x)$ Buy
orders backed by cash $(1+\tau)F_t(x)$ and $S_t(x)$ Sell orders backed
by at least one share. First, we fixed the trade direction using eligible orders at
$F_t(0)$, the price for no net trade. The direction
$d_t$ was positive for net purchases from the dealer, negative for net
sales, and zero for no net trade, as given by:
\begin{equation*}
  d_t=\operatorname{sign}\{B_t(0)-S_t(0)\}.
\end{equation*}
Next, with a nonzero direction fixed, we chose the largest nonnegative
integer size $k$ that left the dealer at least one share and was
supported by the excess eligible orders at its own proposed price.
The selected net purchase $x_t$ was therefore:
\begin{equation}
 x_t=d_t\max\left\{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq0}:
 Q_{t-1}-d_tk\geq1,\quad
 d_t[B_t(d_tk)-S_t(d_tk)]\geq k\right\}.
 \label{eq:dealer-buy-excess}
\end{equation}
We set $x_t=0$ when $d_t=0$. Finally, we allocated orders at the
selected price $F_t(x_t)$, fixing the eligible counts at
$B_t=B_t(x_t)$ and $S_t=S_t(x_t)$. The completed trader buys $n_t^B$
and sells $n_t^S$ were given by:
\begin{equation}
 n_t^B=\min\{B_t,S_t+x_t\},\qquad n_t^S=n_t^B-x_t.
 \label{eq:dealer-sell-excess}
\end{equation}
All eligible orders on the smaller side completed. Orders on a rationed
side were ranked by a reproducible score using simulation seed, step,
trader identity, and side, with identity breaking ties. Eligible orders left unfilled were
\emph{dealer rejection}. Rejection could reflect the minimum share
reserve or a price at which eligible orders no longer supported a
larger net trade, without exhausting dealer cash. The selected net purchase per trader $\phi_t$ summarized completed
orders without separately setting price:
\begin{equation}
 \phi_t=\frac{x_t}{N_{\rm tr}}.
 \label{eq:imbalance}
\end{equation}

All completed orders used the common base execution price
$F_t=F_t(x_t)$, so the dealer's remaining shares $Q_t$ and trading cash
$W_t$ were:
\begin{equation}
 Q_t=Q_{t-1}-x_t,\qquad W_t=W_{t-1}+F_tx_t.
 \label{eq:dealer-ledger}
\end{equation}
After settlement, we set the dealer's quoted price $P_t$ equal to its
remaining trading cash divided by its remaining shares, and substituting
the base execution price into these balances gave:
\begin{equation}
 P_t=\frac{W_t}{Q_t}
     =P_{t-1}\left(\frac{Q_{t-1}}{Q_{t-1}-x_t}\right)^2.
 \label{eq:price-update}
\end{equation}
This quoted price supplied the price signal used by technical traders
and differed from the base execution price whenever $x_t\ne0$. For each
completed order, a buyer paid $(1+\tau)F_t$ and a seller received
$(1-\tau)F_t$, and the dealer collected the fees in a separate cash
account $G_t$, initialized at zero and excluded from the trading
reserves used to set prices:
\begin{equation}
 G_t=G_{t-1}+\tau F_t(n_t^B+n_t^S),\qquad G_0=0.
 \label{eq:dealer-fees}
\end{equation}
No fee was charged on an unfilled order. Cash was conserved across
traders, dealer trading reserves, and the fee account, except at
scheduled changes in trader resources, and shares were conserved across
traders and the dealer, except at scheduled share interventions. Dealer reserves determined both the funding
available for trades and the response of the quoted price to net
trading. The comparison with large reserves used dealer depth $h=100$,
whereas at dealer depth $h=0$ the dealer held no trading cash or shares.
Only opposing trader orders backed by sufficient resources could then be
matched, the price remained at $P_0$, and fees collected in this case
did not fund dealer trading. This separate case therefore provided a
fixed-price control (Algorithm~\ref{alg:dealer-clearing}).

\subsection{Technical traders and price feedback}

Technical traders brought changes in the dealer's quoted price into
neighboring decisions. We selected one quarter of the edge positions uniformly without
replacement for these traders, while the remaining edge positions stayed
in Hold. The technical traders followed a contrarian strategy of
the Bollinger-band type, in which each technical trader compared the
quoted price with a band around a recent price average. A price below
the lower limit generated a Buy intention, a price above the upper limit
generated a Sell intention, and prices within the band, including its
limits, generated Hold. These intentions influenced neighboring interior
traders and supplied orders for the next trading step. This contrarian
response contrasted with the trend-following moving-average rules often
tested in market data~\cite{brock1992simple}. The reference price
$\mu_t$ was an exponentially weighted moving average of the dealer's
quoted price, and the band width used $\sigma_t$, an exponentially
smoothed absolute departure from this average rather than a standard
deviation. Both quantities used the smoothing parameter $\alpha=1/60$.
After settlement, we first updated the average using the new quoted
price $P_t$ and then updated the departure:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \mu_t &= \mu_{t-1}+\alpha(P_t-\mu_{t-1}),\\
 \sigma_t &= \sigma_{t-1}+\alpha\bigl(|P_t-\mu_t|-\sigma_{t-1}\bigr).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:adaptive-band}
\end{equation}
We initialized the average at $\mu_0=P_0$ and the departure at
$\sigma_0=0$. Each technical trader received a fixed multiplier $k_j\sim
U[0.5,1.5]$, giving lower and upper limits $\mu_t-k_j\sigma_t$ and
$\mu_t+k_j\sigma_t$.

At the start of each step, we applied any scheduled changes to decision
rules or trader resources, and traders then submitted orders from their
existing intentions. After settlement, technical traders updated their
bands and intentions using the dealer's new quoted price, and interior
traders then updated simultaneously, using the previous intentions of
neighboring interior traders and the new intentions of adjacent
technical traders. The resulting intentions supplied orders for the
following step (Algorithm~\ref{alg:model-step}). To remove price
feedback, we kept every technical trader in Hold from initialization, so
these traders submitted no orders and communicated Hold to their
neighbors. Interior traders could still trade, and cash, shareholdings,
and the dealer's quoted price could still change, but those changes
could no longer influence interior intentions through the technical
traders. We compared this condition, \emph{technical traders held}, with
\emph{technical traders active}, in which they followed the band rule.
Both conditions began with the same starting market and used the same
interior traders selected for the rule switch, so the comparison tested
whether a Sell majority could develop from responses to neighbors
without feedback from prices. Figure legends abbreviate these conditions
as held and active.
\subsection{Paired rule-switch experiments}

We measured rule-switch effects by pairing switched markets with
no-switch controls that retained the original rule. Each
pair began from the same starting market and used Rule~19280 for every
interior trader for 1,000 steps. At the start of step 1,001, we assigned
Rule~19286 to a preselected fraction $f$ of interior traders, who
retained their current intentions and financial resources, while the
control continued with Rule~19280. The two simulations therefore shared
the same history up to the rule switch. Each condition in the four main
comparisons used 50 starting markets (Supplementary
Table~\ref{tab:campaigns}). Every trader initially held 1,000 cash units
and ten shares, and each simulation continued for 3,000 steps after the
baseline. Additional comparisons tested other decision rules and a
one-time instruction to sell, and we also kept the technical traders'
band widths fixed after the rule switch, tested additional dealer
depths, and extended the observation period. The supplementary protocols
give the conditions and parameter values for these comparisons
(Appendix~\ref{app:revision-controls}).

We varied the fraction of traders given the new rule, market size, and
financial resources to compare the spread of Sell intentions with
changes in completed trades and prices. The rule-switch comparison used
a $64\times64$ grid, active technical traders, and dealer depth $h=100$
at fractions $f\in\{0.10,0.15,0.20,0.25\}$, whereas the dealer-depth
comparison used 11 fractions between 0.10 and 0.25 at eight dealer
depths $h$ from 0 to 100, with technical traders active or held. For the
size comparison, we used grid sizes $L\in\{32,48,64,96,128\}$, with
technical traders held at dealer depth $h=100$ or active at $h=1$ and
$h=100$. At a fixed dealer depth $h$, initial dealer cash and share
reserves increased in proportion to the number of traders. To test how
trader resources affected the response to the rule switch, we
independently multiplied each trader's current cash and shareholdings by
factors $g_w,g_q\in\{0.1,1,10\}$ at the switch, which gave nine
combinations, including unchanged holdings when both factors were 1.
Share counts were rounded to whole numbers, and the intervention did not
rescale the dealer's reserves. With other settings fixed, all nine
simulations shared the same history before the intervention and assigned
the new rule to the same traders. These resource comparisons used
switched markets without separate no-switch controls. When technical
traders were held,
changing resources could alter completed trades and prices but could not
alter interior intentions, because neither cash nor shareholdings
entered the decision rule. The supplementary material gives the full
parameter combinations and verification checks
(Appendix~\ref{app:supplement}).

\subsection{Revision timing and interior technical traders}

Because each trader's next intention ignored its own current state and
all interior traders updated together, the market evolved as two
separate halves. Every neighbor of a trader occupies the opposite color
of a checkerboard, so intentions on the white positions at one step
depended only on intentions on the black positions at the previous step,
and the reverse. The intentions on white positions at even steps and on
black positions at odd steps therefore formed one half of the market,
the remaining intentions formed the other half, and neither half entered
the other's decisions. With technical traders held, the two halves never
influenced each other, whereas with technical traders active they could
influence each other only through the technical traders, whose decisions
depended on a price set by trades from both halves. Updating every
individual at once in a spatial simulation can produce patterns that
change under other update schemes~\cite{huberman1993evolutionary}, so we
repeated the main
comparisons with a revision probability $r$. At each step, each interior
trader adopted its rule's output with probability $r$ and otherwise kept
its current intention, which made its next intention depend on its own
state and joined the two halves, and revision probability $r=1$ recovered
the main model.

Along the edge, technical traders adjoined 1.6\% of interior traders at
grid size $L=64$, so we also placed technical traders on a fraction
$\rho$ of interior positions, in addition to those along the edge
(Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:variants-placement}). Interior technical
traders followed the same band rule with their own multipliers, traded
like other traders, stayed in Hold when technical traders were held,
were never given the new rule, and were excluded from the Sell fraction.
These comparisons used grid size $L=64$ and 50 starting markets, with
technical traders held at dealer depth $h=100$ or active at $h=1$ and
100. We tested revision probabilities $r\in\{1,0.95,0.9,0.75,0.5\}$
without interior technical traders, and interior fractions $\rho$ of
0.025, 0.05, and 0.10, together with 63 interior positions matching the
number along the edge, with revision probability $r=1$. At grid sizes
$L=32$ and 128, we repeated revision probability $r=0.9$ with technical
traders held and interior fraction $\rho=0.05$ with technical traders
held or active at dealer depth $h=100$. Each condition tested 22
fractions $f$ placed around the crossover found in a 20-market pilot. We
estimated the midpoint fraction $f_{50}$ by linear interpolation as in the market-size
comparison, with 95\% intervals from 2,000 bootstrap samples of whole
starting markets drawn once for all conditions, so that differences
between conditions were paired (Appendix~\ref{app:variants}).

\subsection{Following shared advice and copying completed trades}

To examine crowding, we also ran markets in which some traders followed
shared advice or copied another trader's completed trades instead of
responding to their neighbors. These markets used the same $64\times64$
grid, trading, dealer, and technical-trader rules as the rule-switch
experiments, without a rule switch or fees: every interior trader used
Rule~19280 throughout. After the 1,000-step baseline, we chose 10\% or
40\% of the interior traders at random as followers and let them follow
for the next 3,000 steps. Sixteen other interior traders, also chosen at
random, were set aside as traders who could be copied and were counted
neither as followers nor as non-followers. Advice was a random sequence
of Buy, Hold, and Sell recommendations drawn with probabilities 0.25,
0.50, and 0.25 and unrelated to price or to any trader's holdings. Each
recommendation was kept with probability 0.8 at each step and otherwise
drawn again. A follower's intention at each step was the current
recommendation, which replaced the output of its own rule, and
neighboring traders read that intention as they read any other.
Followers' orders still required cash or shares and cleared against the
dealer like every other order. We compared advice from one source shared
by every follower, advice from sixteen sources shared by groups of
followers that differed in size by at most one, and separate advice for each follower. When copying, each
follower repeated the completed trade that its copied trader made in the
previous step, choosing Buy after a completed purchase, Sell after a
completed sale, and Hold otherwise. All followers copied one trader from
the set-aside group, or groups of followers that differed in size by at
most one copied all sixteen.

Each following run was paired with a no-following control that shared
its starting market, baseline history, dealer depth, and technical-trader
condition, and every outcome compared the same traders in the two
markets. To test whether followers' influence on neighboring traders
contributed to their financial outcomes, we repeated the advice runs
with that influence removed: each non-follower read a neighboring
follower's intention from the no-following control, while the followers'
advice and orders were unchanged. With technical traders held, this
left every non-follower decision identical to the no-following control.
In a third comparison, the advice stopped after 1,000 steps, and the
followers returned to Rule~19280 without any change to their cash,
shares, or the price. After the 3,000 steps of following, every trader
with shares sold them to the dealer one share per trader and step, with
no purchases, until no further sale completed. We divided each
follower's final cash by its cash plus shares at the quoted price when
following began, and the follower cash change was the mean of this ratio
minus the same mean for the same traders in the no-following control. We
also measured the change in the fraction of time that non-followers
spent in Sell, over all 3,000 steps and over the final 500, and the
change in the maximum drawdown of the quoted price. The main comparison
used 50 starting markets not used in any other experiment, with two
random assignments of followers and advice per market, dealer depth
$h=1$ and 100, technical traders active or held, and one shared source or
separate advice for each follower, giving 5,200 simulations. Before these
markets were simulated, we fixed two endpoints for 40\% followers of one
shared source at dealer depth $h=100$ with technical traders held: the
follower cash change with advice, and its difference from the run in
which the followers' influence on neighbors was removed. Each endpoint
had to have a positive lower limit on a Bonferroni-adjusted 97.5\%
interval from 10,000 bootstrap resamples of whole starting markets,
computed after averaging the two assignments within each market, and
all other intervals were pointwise 95\% intervals. Sixteen sources and
copying were compared only in an exploratory set of 880 simulations over
10 other starting markets, with 2,000 bootstrap resamples and pointwise
intervals.

\subsection{Market outcomes, statistical analysis, and selection of examples}

We measured intentions and financial outcomes separately because a Sell
majority did not necessarily produce a large price decline. The Sell
fraction $m(t)$ was the fraction of interior traders in Sell after the
intention update at step $t$, and a run reached a Sell majority if $m(t)\geq0.5$ at least once
during the observation period after the rule switch. Onset time was the
first step after the switch at which this threshold was reached. We
estimated majority probability as the fraction of runs reaching the
threshold and summarized onset times only for those runs. For dealer
depth, market size, and trader-resource comparisons, we also averaged
$m(t)$ over the final 500 steps. For the market-size comparison, we
denoted this mean by $\bar m$ and measured its variation between
starting markets using the susceptibility $\chi_L$, the variance of
$\bar m$ multiplied by the number of interior traders,
$(L-2)^2$~\cite{binder1981finite,landau2021guide}. With angle brackets
denoting averages over the 50 starting markets at a fixed grid size and
parameter combination, we calculated:
\begin{equation}
 \chi_L=(L-2)^2\left[\langle\bar m^2\rangle-
                         \langle\bar m\rangle^2\right].
 \label{eq:susceptibility}
\end{equation}
At a fixed grid size, a larger $\chi_L$ indicated greater variation in
the final mean Sell fraction between starting markets
(Eq.~\ref{eq:susceptibility}). For each market size $L$ and condition, we estimated the fractions of
traders assigned the new rule at which majority probability reached
25\%, 50\%, and 75\%, denoted by $f_{25}(L)$, $f_{50}(L)$, and
$f_{75}(L)$. We used straight lines between probabilities at adjacent
tested fractions (linear interpolation), taking the first upward crossing
of each probability level and retaining any decreases in the sampled
probabilities. The midpoint $f_{50}(L)$ located the response, while a
smaller width $f_{75}(L)-f_{25}(L)$ indicated a sharper rise in majority
probability.  We measured price declines using maximum
drawdown, the largest proportional fall from the highest quoted price
reached so far within the observation period, including the quoted price
immediately before the rule switch. We used price crash to describe a
large simulated decline, without a threshold for classifying runs as
crashes. We counted orders lacking cash or shares as trader suppression
and eligible orders left unfilled as dealer rejection. The reported
dealer-rejection fraction was rejected orders divided by eligible orders,
using each run's totals after the rule switch, with zero assigned when no
orders were eligible. Suppressed and completed order counts covered the
same period. We summarized maximum drawdown and dealer-rejection fractions
using medians across starting markets under the same conditions. In the
fee comparisons, majority effects were differences in probabilities,
whereas drawdown effects were medians of the differences within matched
pairs. We used 95\% Wilson
confidence intervals for majority probabilities~\cite{wilson1927probable}.
Where shown, interquartile bands spanned the 25th to 75th percentiles of
outcomes across starting markets under the same conditions.
To compare technical traders active and held, we paired runs by starting
market and counted the markets that reached a majority under only one
of the two conditions. At dealer depths $h=0.1$, 1, and 100, we added
fractions 0.17 and 0.18 from the unchanged-resource runs of the
trader-resource comparison, which reproduced the dealer-depth runs
exactly where the two comparisons overlapped, and counted each run once.
We averaged the paired differences in majority outcome over fixed sets
of fractions and obtained 95\% intervals from 2,000 bootstrap samples
of whole starting markets (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:feedback-pairs}).

For the market-size comparison, we added tested fractions to resolve the rise in majority probability,
aiming for at least five fractions with estimated majority probabilities
strictly between 5\%
and 95\% for each size and condition, allowing up to ten rounds. Each
round added the midpoint of every adjacent pair whose lower fraction had
a probability below 95\% and whose higher fraction had a probability
above 5\%, using the same 50 starting markets at each added fraction
(Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:finite-size-scaling}). For each
market size and condition, we drew 2,000 bootstrap samples of 50 starting
markets with replacement, keeping the same selection at every tested
fraction within a sample. We recalculated $f_{25}$, $f_{50}$, $f_{75}$,
the width $f_{75}-f_{25}$, and the maximum susceptibility over tested
fractions in each sample, using the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles for 95\%
confidence intervals. All requested crossings were bracketed in every
bootstrap sample (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:finite-size-scaling}). To check the sensitivity of these interpolated estimates to the use of
straight lines, we fitted a smooth, increasing logistic probability curve
and compared its midpoint and width at the same probability levels with
the primary estimates. The fitted parameters $a_L$ and $b_L$ controlled
the curve's position and steepness, while 0.18 and 0.01 centered and
scaled the fraction $f$ for fitting. The fitted Sell-majority probability
$p_L(f)$ was given by:
\begin{equation*}
p_L(f)=\left\{1+\exp\left[-a_L-b_L(f-0.18)/0.01\right]\right\}^{-1},
\qquad b_L>0.
\end{equation*}
We estimated the parameters by maximizing the binomial likelihood of the
observed majority counts and refitted the curve in each of the same
2,000 bootstrap samples. Standard errors of its midpoint and width were
the standard deviations of their bootstrap estimates (Supplementary
Table~\ref{tab:revision-fits}). These intervals and standard errors
measured uncertainty from sampling starting markets, conditional on the
tested fractions and observation period. They excluded uncertainty from
selecting additional fractions and, for interpolated estimates, error
from approximating the probability curve by straight lines. A longer
observation period would define a different outcome, whose change these
uncertainty estimates did not describe.

We selected examples of traders' decisions across the grid from the
3,000-step results, before inspecting images, using proximity to group
medians and the lower-numbered starting market to break ties. Selection used
$64\times64$ grids, active technical traders, zero fees,
and the baseline resource settings without cash or share interventions.
At dealer depth $h=100$, we selected a run without a majority at
fraction $f=0.15$, both outcomes at $f=0.175$, and runs with a majority
at $f=0.185$ and 0.20 (Fig.~\ref{fig:dose-panels}). Within each fraction
and outcome group, we chose the run nearest the median onset time for
runs reaching a majority, or nearest the median Sell fraction averaged
over the final 500 steps for runs without a majority. The dealer panels
used a single starting market that reached a majority at dealer depths
$h=0.1$, 1, and 100 for fraction $f=0.20$, chosen to minimize the summed
distance from the median onset among majority-reaching runs at each
depth. The same starting
market also supplied a comparison with technical traders held at dealer
depth $h=100$. We extended the
five examples across fractions to 10,000 steps after the rule switch,
with snapshots at 0, 1,500, 3,000, 5,000, and 10,000 steps
(Fig.~\ref{fig:dose-panels}). The dealer comparisons retained their
3,000-step duration and snapshots at 0, 500, 1,500, and 3,000 steps.
Selection therefore represented typical outcomes within each group over
the original observation period, and a market without a majority at that
endpoint could reach one later. Individual runs illustrated how groups
of sellers developed, while probabilities across runs measured how
often a majority occurred.
