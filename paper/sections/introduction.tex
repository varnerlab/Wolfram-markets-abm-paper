% Introduction

Financial markets exhibit abrupt, large-amplitude crashes that emerge without proportionate
exogenous shocks~\cite{sornette2017why}. The statistical signatures of these events---heavy-tailed
return distributions, volatility clustering, and transient negative autocorrelation---are
remarkably consistent across instruments and time scales~\cite{cont2001empirical,mandelbrot1963variation}.
A central question in quantitative finance is whether such crashes arise endogenously from the
collective dynamics of interacting agents, and if so, what minimal microscopic mechanism is
sufficient to reproduce the observed phenomenology~\cite{battiston2016complexity}.

Agent-based models (ABMs) offer a bottom-up approach to this question. Early work by
Lux and Marchesi~\cite{lux1999scaling} and Bak et al.~\cite{bak1997price} demonstrated that
heterogeneous populations of chartists and fundamentalists can generate fat tails and clustered
volatility. Subsequent models have explored the role of herding~\cite{harras2012noise},
minority-game dynamics~\cite{chowdhury2000statistical}, and network
topology~\cite{iori2002minority} in crash formation. However, most ABMs employ either
fully connected (mean-field) interaction graphs or ad hoc behavioral rules, making it
difficult to disentangle the contributions of local spatial contagion from global coordination.

In this paper we adopt a different strategy: we model agent interactions on a two-dimensional
grid governed by Wolfram totalistic cellular automaton (CA) rules~\cite{wolfram2002new}.
Each agent occupies one cell of the grid and can be in one of three states---Buy, Hold, or
Sell---updated deterministically from the states of its four cardinal neighbors. The CA
framework offers two advantages. First, it enforces strictly \emph{local} information
propagation: sell cascades must spread from neighbor to neighbor, producing spatially
resolved wavefronts that are absent in mean-field models. Second, the full space of
three-state totalistic rules is finite and enumerable ($3^9 = 19{,}683$ rules), enabling
a systematic taxonomy of agent behaviors.

We study two coupled models. In \textbf{Model~A} (open), boundary agents observe real
QQQ ETF minute-bar data via a Bollinger Band filter, injecting exogenous market sentiment
into the grid interior; this validates the CA against empirical stylized facts.
In \textbf{Model~B} (closed), the boundary agents respond to an endogenous price formed
from the grid's own net order imbalance, creating a reflexive feedback loop. The key
finding is a sharp first-order phase transition in crash frequency as a function of the
minority (contrarian) agent fraction~$f$: below a critical threshold
$f_c \approx 0.145$ the market is stable, while above $f_c$ the system abruptly
locks into a sell-dominated regime. The transition is discontinuous and occurs over
$\Delta f < 0.01$, consistent with a tipping-point mechanism
rather than a gradual crossover~\cite{scheffer2009early}.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~2 reviews related work on
ABMs and lattice models in finance. Section~3 introduces the CA rule framework and
edge-agent decision model. Section~4 describes the simulation procedure for Models~A
and~B. Section~5 presents the phase diagram and grid dynamics, and Section~6 concludes.
